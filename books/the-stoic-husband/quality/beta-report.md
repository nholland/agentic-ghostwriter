## Chapter 1 — "The Three-Second Window"

### The Skeptic
**Almost put it down at:** The four-virtue list (Logical / Kind / Self-controlled / Brave enough). This is the first moment the book feels like a self-help listicle. The prose earns its way up to this point — the window concept, the generational sequence, the personal confession — and then suddenly there are four bolded headers and a structured breakdown. The logic is fine. The format says *7 Habits*. What kept me going was the very next sentence: "This isn't a checklist to run mid-fight."

**Would underline:** *"The gap is not a place to ride out the storm at a safe distance. It is the moment before you step toward what's hard. And stay."* — Because this is the thing Stoicism books never say. Every other book in this genre tells men to manage distance. This one names the move explicitly and calls staying the advanced skill. That's the premise earning its keep.

**Felt like filler:** The middle of "The Inward Turn" — the transition from the personal story into the "Am I being the best husband I can be?" question spends two sentences explaining what the question does before asking it. The question is strong enough to stand without the setup.

**Verdict:** *"It's not another Stoicism book. The first chapter does something specific with it. Worth continuing."*

## Chapter 2 — "Stop Outsourcing Your Peace"

### The Skeptic
**Almost put it down at:** The first paragraph. The chapter opens with a four-line block quote from Epictetus, preceded by a two-sentence biography. The previous chapter opened with *you* — inside your experience, right now. This chapter opens with a philosophy lecture. Almost left at line three. A man reading on a plane will skip this paragraph and miss the argument underneath it.

**Verdict:** *"Chapter 1 was better. But the cascade sequence and the corrective section are enough to keep me going."*

### The Domain Expert
**Almost put it down at:** "Preferred indifferent" as a term. The Stoics used *adiaphora* (indifferents) and *proegmena* (preferred indifferents) — both technically accurate. But the explanation here slightly conflates the external object with its evaluation. The object isn't indifferent; *you* are (or should be). Small distinction, but philosophically careful readers will notice.

**Would underline:** The Seneca passage from *De Ira* — *"Reason wishes to give calm to our emotions, not to root them out."* — Correctly cited, well-deployed, does genuine work in the chapter. The application to equanimity-vs.-armor is exactly right.

**Felt like filler:** The "calm sea" vs. "sea legs" metaphor is good but appears three times. "Any man can stand upright when the water is flat" does the work; the next two sentences restate it. Once is enough.

**Verdict:** *"Philosophically solid. The hegemonikon application to marriage is accurate and underexplored elsewhere. The Seneca passage is the best-deployed quote in the manuscript so far."*

## Chapter 3 — "The Discipline of Not Reacting"

### The Skeptic
**Almost put it down at:** "People are rational." The Marcus Aurelius section. Every other philosopher in the manuscript gets a direct quote. Epictetus gets two. Seneca gets one. Marcus Aurelius gets a paraphrase — and the manuscript itself contains an author verification note flagging the citation as unresolved. After two chapters of primary-source direct quotes, presenting Marcus as a paraphrase reads as an unfinished chapter.

**Would underline:** *"She stopped. Not the easy things. The easy things still come. What stops is the harder stuff. The things she's been sitting with. The things that actually matter. You notice she seems a little distant. Nothing happened. She's fine. Just quieter than she used to be."* — Every man reading this knows exactly which version of this silence he's already caused. This is the paragraph that makes a reader buy copies for friends.

**Felt like filler:** "The Record" section. It's doing important work — preventing the reader from swinging to passive agreement — but it arrives compressed and slightly abstract after the clarity of the four-moves section. It's the most thesis-paragraph-shaped section in the manuscript.

**Verdict:** *"The four-move taxonomy is the best thing in these three chapters. I'll finish the book."*

### The Domain Expert
**Almost put it down at:** *"John Gottman spent decades in a research lab watching couples argue. His team could predict which marriages would survive, just from watching how two people fight. They did it with startling accuracy."* — Gottman's predictive accuracy is a real finding (the most-cited studies report ~91% accuracy from 3-minute samples). **Felt like filler:** *"In some cases, a more accurate set."* — The observation that a wife may have a more accurate read of her husband's behavior than he does is one of the most important claims in the chapter. It's buried in a subordinate clause. It should be its own sentence.

*Flagged: The Marcus Aurelius citation (Meditations 9.42 or 11.18) is unresolved in the manuscript as submitted. The author's own note flags it. This is the only chapter of the three that presents a Stoic philosopher without a sourced direct quote. Pre-publication fix required.*

**Verdict:** *"The four-move taxonomy is the manuscript's strongest original contribution. Gottman's number should be in the text. The Marcus Aurelius gap is a pre-publication fix, not an editorial problem."*
