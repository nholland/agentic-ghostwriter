## Why this is filed as a raw source, not a citation

This post repackages real research but is not itself a primary source, and
several of its specific claims turned out to be wrong or outdated on
verification (see `okf/frameworks/the-hedonic-treadmill.md` and the
citation concepts it links to for the corrected versions). This file
preserves what the post actually said, for reference, separate from the
corrected material that made it into the knowledge bundle.

## Core Claims (as stated in the post)

1. Laurie Santos (Yale psychology professor) launched a course in 2018
   ("The Science of Well-Being") that became the most popular class in
   Yale's 300+ year history.
2. Finding 1: things people believe will make them happy (money, the
   right relationship, the bigger house, the better job, the dream body,
   the promotion, recognition) have almost no lasting effect on wellbeing.
3. Mechanism: "hedonic adaptation" — the brain returns to a baseline
   regardless of circumstance. Cited examples: winning the lottery
   (baseline within a year), losing the use of your legs (baseline within
   a year), getting a promotion (baseline within weeks).
4. Named this the "satisfaction treadmill": work harder → get the thing →
   feel better briefly → adapt → need the next thing → treadmill speeds up.
5. Finding 2: what actually produces lasting wellbeing improvement is
   what people systematically deprioritize — strong social connection,
   acts of kindness, fully experiencing ordinary positive moments, sleep,
   physical movement, deliberate gratitude, presence.
6. The gap between knowing this and acting on it is explained by
   "miswanting" — a mispredicting/miscalibrated brain that never updates
   despite consistent evidence that its predictions are wrong. Named
   examples: money's effect "plateaus at a relatively modest income
   level"; wanting and having "activate completely different neural
   systems"; circumstantial change resets to baseline "within months."
7. Practice 1: Savouring — deliberately, fully experiencing a positive
   moment (not photographing/posting/half-experiencing it) long enough
   for the brain to register it. Cross-referenced in the post to Rick
   Hanson's "12-second practice."
8. Practice 2: Concrete replanning — redirecting time from things that
   don't produce wellbeing to things that do (e.g., one hour less on
   phone, one hour more with someone loved).
9. Practice 3: Specific gratitude — not a gratitude list, but a specific
   person, a specific thing they did, a specific impact it had, written
   or spoken directly to them. Claimed: "Santos's research confirmed this
   single practice produces larger and more lasting improvements in
   wellbeing than almost any other intervention ever studied."
10. Closing frame: "You are not broken for wanting the things that do not
    work" — evolutionary mismatch framing (brain calibrated for savanna
    survival, miscalibrated for modern life); the fix is doing what
    already works more consistently.

## Verification Outcome (2026-07-28)

Checked against primary/secondary academic sources before any of this was
added to the OKF bundle. Confirmed accurate: claim 1 (Yale course), the
core of claim 6's miswanting attribution (real term, Santos does teach
it, though she didn't coin it — Gilbert & Wilson, 2000, did), and the
wanting/having neural-systems claim (Berridge & Robinson's incentive-
salience research). Wrong or unsupported as stated: "satisfaction
treadmill" is not a real term Santos or the field uses (the actual term,
predating her by decades, is the "hedonic treadmill," coined by Brickman
& Campbell, 1971); the "lose the use of your legs, within a year,
baseline" claim overstates the real 1978 study's finding (accident
victims scored meaningfully lower than controls, not equal); the money
plateau claim reflects a 2010 finding since complicated by a 2021 study
and a 2023 resolution; and the "larger and more lasting" claim about
specific gratitude inverts what its own likely source study (Seligman et
al., 2005) actually found — that practice had the largest immediate
effect of the exercises tested, but was one of the least durable, while a
different exercise ("Three Good Things") was the one that actually lasted
at six months. See the corrected framework and its linked citations for
the versions actually used in the book.
