## Core Claims

1. People judged by close others (spouses, family members, close friends) as more morally good tend to report greater meaning in life
2. The same group often reports greater happiness — though the relationship with happiness is somewhat more variable than the relationship with meaning
3. The moral traits used in the research are specifically relevant to marriage: compassion, respectfulness, honesty, fairness, loyalty, dependability
4. The implication: being a "good person" in the conventional Stoic sense is not a sacrifice of happiness — it may be a path to it

## Frameworks or Concepts Introduced

- **Close-other ratings of moral character**: the methodology of assessing moral goodness through the eyes of people who know the subject well (spouse, close friends) — particularly relevant because it measures character as it actually shows up in relationship, not just self-reported
- **Meaning vs. happiness distinction**: the article apparently distinguishes between these two outcomes, with morality more strongly predicting meaning than happiness per se

## Relationship to Book Thesis

**Directly supports the book's central counter-intuitive argument.**

The book argues that the path to a good marriage runs through becoming a better person, not through getting more of what you want. This article provides modern psychological backing for that ancient Stoic claim.

It also answers the reader's implicit objection: *"If Stoicism requires constant self-sacrifice and virtue, why would I want that? Won't I just be the doormat who gives everything and gets nothing?"*

The answer from this research: the man who becomes more honest, loyal, fair, compassionate, and dependable is not sacrificing happiness. He is building the conditions that make durable marital happiness possible. And the people best positioned to rate his moral character — his close others, beginning with his wife — are exactly the ones whose judgment determines the quality of his marriage.
