# Things We Tolerate

The author's own, in his words:

- **Political disagreement, which no longer waits for Thanksgiving.** It used
  to be one table once a year. Now friends and family are in front of you
  constantly, and some of them get zealous and push it over the top.
- **Friends of hers who are not friendly to the relationship.** Not
  anti-relationship — they simply say things that are not helpful. He tolerates
  it because he wants his partner to have good friends, and that is worth
  more than the friction.
- **Family members who struggle with money**, whom they help from time to
  time.
- **Family members with different ideas about raising kids.**

# Why These Work For The Chapter

Each one has a visible reason underneath, which is what separates tolerating
from swallowing. The friends case is the strongest of the four: he can name
exactly what he is buying with his tolerance, and it is something he actually
wants. That is the test the beat should teach — not *can you put up with it*,
but *can you say what you are getting for it*.

The political example also does useful work for scope. It is the one that is
not about the marriage at all, which quietly makes the point that most of what
a man is irritated by on a given day was never his marriage's problem.

# Handling

Pick two, not four. `04-archetype.md` sets citation and example density low,
and this is the chapter's lighter passage — a list of four starts to read as
an inventory. The friends case and one family case is enough.
