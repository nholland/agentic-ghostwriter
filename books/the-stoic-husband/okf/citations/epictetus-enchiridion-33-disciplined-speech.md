# Epictetus — Enchiridion 33: Disciplined, Not Total, Silence

Epictetus advises: keep silence for the most part, or say only what is
necessary and in few words. When occasion genuinely calls for speech, speak —
but not about trivial, common subjects, and above all not to blame, praise,
or compare other people.

# Why this fits Chapter 9

This passage is usually reached for as evidence that Stoicism recommends
staying quiet — exactly the misapplication this chapter needs to correct
before a reader uses it to justify never voicing a want. Read in full,
Epictetus isn't silencing the self; he's disciplining *which* speech is worth
spending — restraint from gossip, complaint, and reactive noise, which is a
different thing entirely from concealing a genuine position from the one
person who most needs to hear it. This gives Chapter 9's self-control beat
(the Virtue Question's temperance pillar) its Stoic anchor: the filter isn't
"say nothing," it's "say what's actually worth saying, and mean it when you
do."
