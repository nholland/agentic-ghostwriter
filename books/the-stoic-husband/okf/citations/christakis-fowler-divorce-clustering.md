# McDermott, Fowler & Christakis (2013) — Divorce Clustering Through Social Networks

Using the Framingham Heart Study's decades-long social network data, the
authors find divorce clusters among friends: a close friend's divorce is
associated with a 75% increase in one's own odds of divorcing, and the
effect extends to two degrees of separation — a friend-of-a-friend's
divorce still measurably raises the odds, even with no direct relationship
to that person. Presence of children doesn't change the odds of divorce
itself, but each additional child reduces susceptibility to peer influence.

# Why this was a candidate for Chapter 10 (retained for context)

This is the empirical backbone for the chapter's Wisdom beat: the test for
whether a given friendship is actually corrosive isn't vibes, it's whether
proximity to that friend's attitudes and choices measurably shifts a man's
own. This paper is evidence that marriage-adjacent norms really do transmit
through a social network, not just an intuition the chapter is asserting.

# Caution

This is a contested finding, not settled science. The same statistical
technique Christakis and Fowler used for their well-known obesity-contagion
and happiness-contagion papers has been publicly challenged (Russell Lyons
and others have argued the methods overstate causal social-contagion
effects). Use language that reflects genuine research findings under debate
("researchers who study social networks have found..."), not certainty, and
prefer the plain claim (friend groups shape norms and behavior) over the
specific contested percentages unless those percentages are confirmed
against the primary paper.
