# Marcus Aurelius — "If It's Endurable, Then Endure It" (Meditations 10.3)

> "Everything that happens is either endurable or not. If it's endurable, then
> endure it. Stop complaining."
> — *Meditations* 10.3, Hays trans. (wording to be confirmed)

# Why This Is Ch11's Anchor

The chapter argues that a man must first work out what kind of hard he is
facing, because the required response differs by kind. Marcus does exactly
that here. He does not open with "bear it." He opens with a **sort**, and only
then instructs. That structural match is why this passage beats any of the
better-known fortitude lines: it models the chapter's method, not just its
conclusion.

# Handling Note

`02-audience.md`'s reader already believes Stoicism means not being affected
(belief #1). "Stop complaining" handed to that reader confirms the misread on
contact. Strip the armor reading before using it: Marcus is not banning the
feeling. He is banning the running commentary about a thing already
established as unavoidable — which is a narrow instruction, and only applies
once the sort has been done honestly.

Quote the first three sentences. The continuation about destruction is
powerful and belongs to a different argument than this chapter is making.
