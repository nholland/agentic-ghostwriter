# Epictetus, Enchiridion 33 — Lay Down Your Character in Advance

Epictetus opens the passage by telling his student to settle, at the outset,
what kind of man he is going to be — and then to hold that same character
alone and in company alike. The instruction is pre-commitment: the decision
is made in advance, in calm, and what remains in the moment is only the
keeping of it.

# Why this is Chapter 10's spine

The rebuilt Chapter 10 argues that boundaries are strength, not distance.
This passage supplies the mechanism underneath that claim. A boundary
improvised under pressure is a preference — it folds, because it was never
decided. A boundary laid down beforehand is character, and holding it in the
group chat, at the in-laws' table, and in the middle of an argument is a
single discipline rather than three.

It also speaks directly to the chapter's target reader: the man who has never
consciously realized that deciding these lines is his job. Epictetus doesn't
tell him which character to choose. He tells him that choosing is the work,
and that not choosing is itself a choice already made.

# The Chapter 9 relationship — a callback, not a repeat

Chapter 9 used *Enchiridion* 33's teaching on disciplined, selective silence,
and corrected a misreading of it ("that's not the whole point Epictetus was
making"). In Long's rendering, the silence instruction is the **second
sentence of the same passage** — it follows immediately after the character
instruction quoted here. Chapter 10 can therefore open by going back one
line: *last chapter I told you his rule about silence was never about hiding
things from your wife; here's the sentence that comes right before it.*

This converts what the research pass first flagged as a cross-chapter reuse
risk (see `.claude/OKF.md`, chapter references; CLAUDE.md Step 2.5 reuse
check) into the chapter's opening device — and satisfies `04-archetype.md`'s
rule against repeating a consecutive chapter's opening type, since a callback
(type 3) is not Ch9's direct-address open (type 1).

# Correction to This Concept's Own Notes (2026-09-01)

External verification confirmed the Long wording exactly, and in doing so
caught an error in what this file previously recorded about other
translations. **Matheson (1916) does not use the "Immediately prescribe"
wording.** Matheson begins "Lay down for yourself from the first" and speaks of
a definite stamp and style of conduct; Oldfather (Loeb, 1928) likewise begins
"Lay down for yourself, at the outset."

The passage is genuine and Ch10 quotes it accurately. What was wrong was this
concept's comparison note, which would have misled anyone later choosing
between editions.
