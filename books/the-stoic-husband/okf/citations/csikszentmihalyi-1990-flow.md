# Csikszentmihalyi (1990), Flow

**One-sentence gloss the Researcher can stand behind (paraphrase; no quotation marks):** The
psychologist Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi used "flow" for the state in which you are so absorbed in
something that stretches you, without overwhelming you, that you stop watching yourself and the
doing becomes its own reward.

**What can be said in print:** Csikszentmihalyi named flow and made it famous in *Flow: The
Psychology of Optimal Experience* (1990). Name him and the book; gloss the idea in plain English;
quote nothing.

# How the author's use differs (for the brief; his words from Round 6)

- The author's definition: "Flow is effectively when everything is going well: you're at peace and
  you're operating at your highest self." The second half ("operating at your highest self") is
  close to Csikszentmihalyi's absorbed, fully engaged state. The first half ("at peace") is the
  author's emphasis, not Csikszentmihalyi's: flow in the book is effortful and stretched, not
  restful. Prose should give the author's sense as his ("the way I use it") rather than attribute
  "at peace" to Csikszentmihalyi.
- Flow is a concept about one person's experience of an activity. Applying it to a marriage as one
  unit, and saying the three Stoic words "are representative of flow", is the author's step, and
  the prose should say so. Caution: do NOT write that Csikszentmihalyi never applied flow to
  relationships. Summaries report a section on flow and the family in Chapter 8 (unconfirmed);
  the safe sentence is that the application here is the author's own.
- "Popular in business" (author) is fair as his observation; the book was not written as a
  business book, so do not say it was.

# Cautions

- Never transcribe a sentence of Csikszentmihalyi from memory or from a search snippet. The
  well-known lines that circulate online are exactly the kind of wording search returns fluently
  and wrongly.
- No numbers. No "studies show". If a desk wants the family subsection, it needs the book in hand.
- The word "flow" is already the River's in this book (`00-premise.md`: "he flows instead of
  fighting the current"; Introduction: "When life changes, flow." and "Flow with wisdom."). See
  the Ch16 brief (runs/ch16/research.md) for how that collision is handled; it is a book decision,
  not a citation fact.
- Possible classical bridge (separate source, separate concept): Diogenes Laertius VII.88 records
  the Stoic phrase usually rendered "a smooth flow of life" (Hicks: "the smooth current of life")
  beside virtue and happiness. See
  [/citations/diogenes-laertius-7-87-90-eudaimonia-and-arete.md](/citations/diogenes-laertius-7-87-90-eudaimonia-and-arete.md).
  It is not Csikszentmihalyi's source and must not be presented as one.
