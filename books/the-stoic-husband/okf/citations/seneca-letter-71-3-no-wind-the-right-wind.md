# Seneca, Letter 71.3 (Gummere): no wind is the right wind

**What it says, plainly:** If you do not know where you are going, nothing that happens can help
you get there. Decide the aim first.

# Wording (as read at page-text, Gummere 1920, p. 75)

> When a man does not know what harbour he is making for, no wind is the right wind.

Context, same section: "The archer must know what he is seeking to hit; then he must aim and
control the weapon by his skill. Our plans miscarry because they have no aim." (Read at the same
page; the archer is a second image in the same breath.)

# Fit for Ch16 (optional; the outline lists it as optional)

The author's sense of it: when she is upset, he stays calm because he knows where he wants the
marriage to go. That is his application, not Seneca's subject (Letter 71 is about the supreme good
as the aim of a whole life).

# Cautions

- **Image collision.** Ch16's one image is the bank. A harbour, wind and sailing would be a second
  concrete image (01-voice.md: never stack metaphors). The Introduction gives the storm to the Oak
  ("Its roots go deeper than the storm"). If used at all, a single clause, or the idea in the
  author's plain words with Seneca named and no quotation.
- Do not print "knowing where you want to go in the storm" as a Stoic quotation.
