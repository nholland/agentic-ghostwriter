# Epictetus, Enchiridion 30 — Duties Are Measured by Relations

Epictetus's method here is to derive obligation from relationship rather than
from the other person's merit. Being someone's son implies a set of duties;
the objection *but he is a bad father* does not dissolve them, because the tie
was never to a good father, only to a father. The same move is run for
brothers, neighbors, citizens, and commanders: consider not what he does, but
what you are to do.

# Why this fits Chapter 10

Two beats need it.

**Tier 1's hardest pressure.** The author's own framing of the chapter names
the difficulty plainly: it is hard to honor a shared boundary when your
partner isn't honoring it herself. Epictetus is the answer. The line you laid
down is yours to keep, and its force does not run through her conduct. This
is the difference between a boundary and a bargain — a bargain is void when
the other side defaults; a boundary isn't, because it was never a transaction.

**Tier 3's in-laws.** What a husband owes his father-in-law is set by the
relation, which is real and does not disappear because the man is
overstepping. He still gets courtesy, patience, and a direct conversation. He
does not get a vote in how the couple runs their family. Epictetus lets the
chapter hold both at once, which is what keeps the tier from reading as
permission to go to war with relatives.
