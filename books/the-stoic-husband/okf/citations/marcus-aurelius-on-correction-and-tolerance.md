# Marcus Aurelius — Bad Breath, Tolerance, and the Virtue of Being Wrong (Meditations 5.28)

> "Art thou angry with him whose armpits stink? Art thou angry with him whose
> mouth smells foul? What good will this anger do thee? He has such a mouth,
> he has such armpits: it is necessary that such an emanation must come from
> such things." — Meditations 5.28 (translation to be confirmed)

# Two Readings

**1. The Tolerance Reading (Marcus's original point).** Don't be angry at
people for what simply emanates from their nature — the smell is a fact
about them, not a provocation aimed at you. If you can help them correct it
gently, do; otherwise, don't waste anger on a biological fact.

**2. The Reflection Reading (the modern "virtue of being wrong" twist,
commonly associated with Donald Robertson's writing on Stoicism).** Flip the
analogy inward. If a friend told you — even bluntly — that your breath
smelled bad, you'd be embarrassed for a moment, then grateful: they revealed
a blind spot you couldn't smell yourself. A Stoic treats moral feedback
("you're being lazy / selfish / short with me") the same way: as useful data
about a blind spot, not as an attack to be defended against. Socrates'
framing applies here too — to be shown you're wrong is a benefit, because
you've been cured of an error.

# Why This Resolves the Ch6 Placeholder

The author's "Am I lazy?" reflection in the laundry scene is, almost
word-for-word, Reading 2. When his wife says "I knew you wouldn't raise a
finger," the Stoic move isn't to get angry (Reading 1, applied to himself —
don't let anger be the first response) or to recite the scoreboard in
self-defense. It's to actually check: is this true? If yes — be grateful for
the blind spot, and act on it. If no — say so plainly, without anger, the way
Reading 1 says not to be angry at someone's "emanation." Either way, the
ledger never opens.

This gives Ch6 a single Marcus Aurelius passage that does double duty: it can
frame how the husband receives criticism (Reading 2, the core mechanism) and,
more briefly, how he might regard a sharp comment from his wife in the moment
(Reading 1) — without needing two separate citations.
