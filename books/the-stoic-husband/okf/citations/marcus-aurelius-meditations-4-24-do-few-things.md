# Meditations IV.24 — Occupy Thyself With Few Things

Long's text, read 2026-09-16 from Gutenberg #15877:

> 24. Occupy thyself with few things, says the philosopher, if thou wouldst be
> tranquil.--But consider if it would not be better to say, Do what is
> necessary, and whatever the reason of the animal which is naturally social
> requires, and as it requires. For this brings not only the tranquillity
> which comes from doing well, but also that which comes from doing few
> things. For the greatest part of what we say and do being unnecessary, if a
> man takes this away, he will have more leisure and less uneasiness.
> Accordingly, on every occasion a man should ask himself, Is this one of the
> unnecessary things?

# What Changed From Round 1

Round 1 could not reach the text and ruled that the chapter print no
quotation. That ruling is no longer forced by the evidence. The text is in
hand, in the house translator, in the public domain. Whether to quote it is
now an editorial choice rather than an evidence limit — and 06-sources.md's
own caveat still applies ("where a Long passage would fight the register
badly, prefer rendering the idea in the author's own words"). Long's
thee/thou register is exactly the case that caveat describes, and the chapter
currently carries the idea in plain English and reads well.

The sentence the chapter could now legitimately quote, if the author wants
old language before the plain translation, is the short one:
*"Occupy thyself with few things, says the philosopher, if thou wouldst be
tranquil."* It contains no dash and needs no splice.

# Why It Fits Ch12

Ordering under scarcity. The cutting is going to happen whether or not he
decides; the only question is whether he picks the list. That is the same
claim as the grandfather's "put your wife first," in Stoic terms.
