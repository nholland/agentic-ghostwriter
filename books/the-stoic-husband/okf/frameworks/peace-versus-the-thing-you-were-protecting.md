# Peace Versus the Thing You Were Protecting

Children complain, and they don't complain once. They complain on a Tuesday
when you were already finished at four. There is a version of that night where
you give in, and the story you tell yourself is that you're picking your
battles.

That isn't what happened. You traded the thing you were protecting for an hour
of quiet. The trade is understandable and almost universal, and it is still a
trade.

The second-order cost is the one worth naming. When one partner takes that
trade and the other doesn't, the argument stops being between the parents and
the child and becomes an argument between the parents. One of them wanted peace
in the house more than they wanted the thing the two of them had agreed to
protect. That is rarely how either of them would describe it in the moment,
which is exactly why it keeps happening.

# Why this matters for Chapter 10

This is the chapter's enforcement beat in one concrete scene. The chapter's
first half argues that a boundary is only real if you can name what it
protects; this names the moment where that naming gets tested, and it is not a
dramatic confrontation with an in-law. It's an ordinary weeknight with a tired
parent and a kid who won't stop.

It also keeps the enforcement material honest. A chapter that only showed
boundaries being tested by *other people* (in-laws, friends, family asking for
money) would let the reader off: the pressure is always external, and he is
always the one holding firm. Here the pressure is his own exhaustion, and the
person who gives the boundary away is him.
