# Temperament Asymmetry (Who Cares More Wins)

# Schema

**Reactivity asymmetry.** Across any stretch of shared life — a day, a
week, a month — some number of situations could reasonably provoke a
reaction. A highly reactive partner has a strong opinion about a much
larger share of them than a low-reactive partner does. Because outcomes
tend to follow whoever cares more, not whoever's actually right, the
reactive partner's preference wins by default in most of those
situations — not through better judgment, but through emotional volume.
The calm partner's acquiescence looks like peacekeeping. It's actually a
tax paid, quietly, for the marriage's temperature.

**Industriousness asymmetry.** One partner's baseline bar for "enough
work done" sits much higher than the other's. The industrious partner
works until exhausted; the other partner's version of "enough" includes
real rest, maybe more than the industrious partner would ever allow
themselves. Neither bar is inherently wrong. But the industrious partner
ends up doing more, not because anyone assigned it that way, but because
their own threshold for stopping sits higher than their partner's.

# The shared mechanism

Both are spectrum traits, not incidents. Nobody decided, in a single
moment, that the reactive partner would get their way 8 times out of 10,
or that the industrious partner would carry more of the load. It emerges
automatically from two different baselines meeting each other, repeated
across hundreds of small moments. This makes it harder to name than
[Four Types of Unfairness](/frameworks/four-types-of-unfairness.md)'s
Input/Output type, because there's no single ask or answer to point to.
The pattern is only visible in aggregate — across 10 situations, or a
year — never in any one of them.

# The tension this creates with the book's own advice

Chapters 1–5 teach a man to be the calm one: to not react by default, to
hold his own weather regardless of hers. Read naively, that's exactly the
posture that loses every negotiation to a more reactive partner in the
pattern above. The correction: calm was never supposed to mean silent, or
without a position. [The Dichotomy of Control in Marriage](/frameworks/dichotomy-of-control-in-marriage.md)
already separates not reacting emotionally from not acting at all — a
Stoic husband governs his own reaction, then still advocates for what's
actually fair. Whatever chapter carries this framework's full weight needs
to make that correction explicit, or it risks undermining the book's own
Part I thesis.
