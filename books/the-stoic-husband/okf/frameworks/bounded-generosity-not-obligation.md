# Schema

Four research threads, synthesized into one argument.

**1. Initiation can precede desire (Basson, 2000).**
Spontaneous desire — wanting sex out of nowhere — is common early in a
relationship. **Responsive desire**, which arises *after* arousal or
closeness has already begun, is the norm in long-term marriages. This
means a partner's overture or initiation isn't skipping a required
step — for many people, most of the time, it *is* the step that produces
the feeling. This is the legitimate version of "engage and the feeling
follows" — grounded in real sexual-response research, not a scheduling
gimmick.

**2. Roughly weekly is where the data plateaus, not a quota (Muise,
Schimmack & Impett, 2016).**
Across three large studies, the link between sexual frequency and
well-being is curvilinear: it rises up to about once a week, then flattens.
More frequency past that point isn't associated with more well-being. This
is useful as a **reference point** for what a healthy baseline tends to
look like on average — it is not evidence that a specific number is owed
in a specific marriage, and it says nothing about what to do when partners
disagree about frequency.

**3. The answer to asymmetric desire is bounded generosity, not
obligation and not total refusal (Impett & Peplau, 2005; Muise, Impett,
Kogan & Desmarais, 2013).**
This is the load-bearing distinction. Two people can both "have sex without
their own desire present" for completely different reasons, with opposite
results:

- **Avoidance-motivated compliance** — engaging to prevent a fight or a
  partner's disappointment. Predicts *more* conflict, *less* closeness,
  *less* satisfaction. Partners who do this often report the **lowest**
  relationship satisfaction of any group studied. This is what "I'm not
  doing this out of obligation" is rightly refusing.
- **Sexual communal strength** — engaging out of care for the partner's
  wellbeing, an approach motive rather than an avoidance motive. Predicts
  *sustained* desire over time and better outcomes for both partners, even
  extended specifically to couples where one partner has chronic low
  interest.
- **The caveat that keeps this from becoming a loophole**: "unmitigated
  communion" — always prioritizing the partner's needs to the total
  exclusion of one's own — predicts *worse* sexual well-being and more
  distress. The generosity has to be bounded on both sides, not
  unconditional.

The practical upshot: the question isn't "will you do this or not" — it's
"can this come from care rather than either fear-of-conflict or total
refusal." That's a real, different question, and it's the one worth
having out loud.

**4. Desire mismatch is a feature of long marriages, not a bug (Herbenick
et al., 2014; Vowels & Mark, 2020).**
Desire discrepancy is one of the most common reasons couples enter
therapy — not a sign of a broken bond. What predicts better outcomes isn't
resolving the mismatch, it's **talking about it directly** rather than
either forcing a number or avoiding the subject entirely. Over half of
couples who manage this well report communication, not compliance, as
their actual strategy.

**What this explicitly rules out:** a framing where a husband is told to
push for a fixed quota regardless of his wife's state, and a framing where
"I don't want to, and that's obligation" becomes an unexaminable trump
card that forecloses the conversation. Both skip the actual work, which is
naming the mismatch honestly and finding what each partner can offer from
care rather than fear or resignation.
