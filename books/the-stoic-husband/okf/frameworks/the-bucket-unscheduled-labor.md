# The Bucket (Unscheduled Labor)

Every marriage splits its labor, on purpose or by drift, and most of that
split has a name and a rhythm: trash on Tuesday, dishes most nights. That
named, scheduled work is visible almost by construction — it happens often
enough, and predictably enough, that both partners eventually register it
exists, even if they don't fully credit how much it costs.

Most of what actually causes resentment isn't that kind of work. It's the
rattling ceiling fan that's needed attention for a month. The one-off
project. Gassing up both cars and running them through the wash. Coaching
the team because nobody else would. None of these repeats often enough to
earn its own slot on a calendar or a name anyone would use in conversation.
They all live in the same unnamed place: a bucket labeled "whatever needs
doing."

# Schema

1. **Named/scheduled labor is self-reinforcing-visible.** Because it
   repeats on a predictable rhythm, both partners build an internal model
   of it existing, even without consciously tracking it.
2. **Bucket labor is structurally invisible, not carelessly overlooked.**
   There's no recurring moment that prompts either partner to notice the
   bucket got emptied — it isn't forgetfulness or lack of appreciation,
   it's the absence of any repeating cue that would trigger noticing at
   all.
3. **The asymmetry compounds over time, not just at any one moment.**
   Named labor's visibility doesn't erode with time (Tuesday's trash is
   just as noticeable in year ten as year one). Bucket labor's invisibility
   gets worse the longer a marriage runs, because the bucket grows (more
   one-off things accumulate in a longer-running household) while the
   mechanism that would make any single item visible never develops — there's
   no threshold effect that eventually makes bucket labor "count" the way
   repetition does for named labor.

# Why this isn't just "invisible labor" restated

The visible/invisible labor split is a real, separately-documented pattern
(see [Gillespie, Peterson & Lever (2019)](/citations/gillespie-peterson-lever-2019-fairness-housework-expenses.md)
for the external research anchor). This framework is more specific than
that split: it's not about *cognitive* invisibility (the mental load of
planning, remembering, coordinating) but about a particular *structural*
reason certain *physical* tasks go uncounted — the absence of a repeating
schedule. A task can be entirely physical, one-time, and still fall into
the bucket purely because it doesn't recur often enough to become a named
category either partner tracks.
