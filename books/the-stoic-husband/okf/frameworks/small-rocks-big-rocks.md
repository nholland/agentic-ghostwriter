# Small Rocks, Big Rocks (Incompatible Scoreboards)

**Correction (2026-06-28):** This framework originally mapped "small rocks"
to visible chores and "big rocks" to invisible cognitive load. The author
corrected this during Ch7 drafting: the mapping runs the other way, and
matches the classic jar metaphor (big rocks first, then pebbles, then sand
fills the rest of the volume).

- **Big rocks** are the visible, finished, nameable contributions: the
  deck you rebuilt, the financial crisis you solved, the day you took off
  work. They have a beginning, an end, and a story attached. They're also
  the only thing either spouse can actually cite in an argument, because
  they're the only thing memorable enough to retrieve on demand.
- **Small rocks** are the constant, individually-trivial, mostly invisible
  motions: the question you didn't ask because you knew it'd start a
  fight, the five minutes spent finding her keys, noticing she hadn't
  eaten. No single one is worth mentioning. But they are not rare. They
  are the actual volume in the jar, the bulk of what either person
  contributes day to day.

The insight isn't visible-vs-invisible labor (that's a real but separate
point already covered by fairness research on housework/expenses — see
`/okf/citations/gillespie-peterson-lever-2019-fairness-housework-expenses.md`).
The insight is that **scorekeeping can only audit big rocks**, because
small rocks are too numerous and too individually small for either person,
including the person who dropped them, to consciously track. Any ledger
either spouse keeps is therefore auditing the smallest fraction of what's
actually happening in the marriage and calling it the whole picture.

# Schema

| Category | Examples | Countable in an argument? | Share of total volume |
|---|---|---|---|
| Big rocks | The deck, the financial save, the day off work | Yes — memorable, citable | Small |
| Small rocks | Finding her keys, not asking the question that'd start a fight, noticing she hadn't eaten | No — too numerous, too small individually | Large (the bulk) |

# Connects to the 60/40 rule

This is the structural reason "give 60%, expect 40%" (author's grandfather's
marriage advice) works as practical guidance rather than just a nice
sentiment: anyone trying to get full credit for everything they actually
contribute is trying to get credit for hundreds of small rocks nobody was
ever going to count, including themselves. Aiming to give more than you
expect back is the only stance that survives contact with a ledger that
can't see most of what's on it.
