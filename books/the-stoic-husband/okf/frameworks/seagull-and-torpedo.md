# Seagull and Torpedo

**The seagull** flies in, makes a lot of noise, dumps on everything, and
leaves. Nothing good ever comes out of his mouth, only corrections. In a
marriage he is the man with a running commentary on everything he does not
like, delivered as it occurs to him, at whatever volume he happens to be at.

**The torpedo** holds. Then holds. Then holds. Then detonates over something
that did not deserve it.

Most men correcting for the seagull become the torpedo, because the only fix
they can see for saying too much is saying nothing. That is the trap this
chapter is built to close.

# Why It Belongs Here

It gives the chapter its second failure mode. Without it, the argument
collapses into "speak up more," and a reader who has met a seagull knows
perfectly well that more talking is not automatically better. Naming both
ends makes the actual claim visible: the variable is not volume, it is
**timing and posture** — small, early, kind, and not requiring anything back.

# Attribution Note

**"Seagull management" is not the author's coinage and must not be presented
as one.** The term was popularized by Ken Blanchard, who used it in a 1984
interview ("A British friend of mine calls it the seagull-type of management.
The big guy flies in, dumps on everybody, and is gone") and in *Leadership and
the One Minute Manager* (1985). Blanchard himself credited an unidentified
British friend, so the coinage is genuinely unattributable further back. If
the chapter uses the term it should say, in plain prose, that it comes from
management writing — a sentence is enough.

**"Torpedo manager" is the author's own pairing** and is not an established
management term. Do not attribute it to Blanchard or to anyone else; either
present it as the author's own name for the reverse case, or use a plain
description instead.
