# The Hedonic Treadmill (and Miswanting)

A man can win the achievement, get the promotion, hit the number he's
been chasing — and find that the lift doesn't last. That isn't a
character flaw or a sign he chose the wrong goal. It's what the brain
does to every goal, good or bad, by design.

# Schema

1. **The hedonic treadmill.** Coined by Brickman & Campbell (1971): large
   life changes, positive or negative, produce only temporary shifts in
   wellbeing before adaptation pulls a person back toward a baseline
   level. The classic empirical test (Brickman, Coates & Janoff-Bulman,
   1978) compared lottery winners, controls, and accident victims left
   paralyzed: winners were not happier day-to-day than controls, and took
   *less* pleasure from ordinary events than either other group.
   Accident victims were meaningfully less happy than controls — not
   fully bounced back to baseline, the honest reading of that data — but
   far closer to it, and far more hopeful about the future, than most
   people predict. Both directions confirm the same mechanism: the
   circumstance changes; the felt experience of it fades faster than
   expected, whether the change was a windfall or a loss.

2. **The loop.** Chase the thing believed to produce happiness. Get it.
   Feel better, briefly. Adapt. Need the next thing. The loop doesn't
   slow down as it repeats — the bar for "enough" moves with it, so the
   distance between "where I am" and "the thing that will finally satisfy
   me" never actually closes.

3. **Why it persists: miswanting.** Named by Gilbert & Wilson (2000) —
   Santos teaches this exact term in her course; she didn't originate it.
   The brain's affective-forecasting system fails in specific, identified
   ways: imagining the wrong future event, using the wrong theory of what
   produces happiness, and misinterpreting its own feelings once the
   event arrives. The predictable result is inflating both the intensity
   and the *duration* of the good feeling a goal will produce. The
   failure isn't random — it's the same three errors, repeating.

4. **A physiological piece of the same story: wanting vs. liking.**
   Berridge & Robinson's research shows that the neural circuitry driving
   the *pursuit* of a reward (dopamine-driven "wanting") is a different,
   larger, more robust system than the circuitry that produces the actual
   pleasure of *having* it ("liking," a smaller, more fragile,
   non-dopamine system). This gives the treadmill a mechanism, not just a
   pattern: the anticipation and the payoff are quite literally not the
   same brain event, so the intensity of the chase never reliably
   predicts the size of the reward.

5. **The complicating case: money.** Resist the flat claim that money's
   effect on happiness "plateaus at a modest income" — that's the 2010
   Kahneman & Deaton finding (~$75,000), directly contradicted by
   Killingsworth (2021), and resolved by a 2023 adversarial collaboration
   between the two: for most people, happiness keeps rising with income;
   only the least happy fifth of people show a plateau, around $100,000.
   The honest version of this point is narrower than the treadmill
   framing wants it to be — money is not nothing, and for some people it
   keeps mattering further than intuition suggests. Use this case as a
   caution against over-claiming the mechanism, not as another treadmill
   example.

6. **What actually produces durable wellbeing.** Strong social
   connection, kindness toward others, deliberately savoring ordinary
   positive moments, sleep, physical movement, and specific (not vague)
   gratitude. Not because these "feel" more important than achievement —
   because they're the inputs the brain doesn't habituate to nearly as
   fast.

7. **Two practices, honestly characterized:**
   - **Savoring** — Rick Hanson's "taking in the good" (the HEAL method:
     Have a positive experience, Enrich it, Absorb it, Link it): staying
     with a positive moment for several extra seconds, on purpose,
     rather than letting it pass unregistered — not photographing it,
     not posting it, not half-experiencing it while already thinking
     about the next thing. The mechanism (countering the brain's
     negativity bias, which encodes threats faster than it encodes good
     moments) is well-supported. A specific duration (the "12-second"
     figure sometimes attached to this practice) is not confirmed against
     any primary source found during verification — describe it as "long
     enough to actually register," not a fixed count.
   - **Specific gratitude, two forms with different payoffs** (Seligman,
     Steen, Park & Peterson, 2005): naming a specific person, a specific
     thing they did, and its specific impact — written or spoken directly
     to them — is the "gratitude visit," and it produced the single
     largest immediate effect of the five interventions tested in that
     study (~10% happiness increase, ~35% drop in depressive symptoms).
     But it faded within weeks and was gone within three to six months.
     The exercise that was still measurable at six months was a
     different one — "Three Good Things" (writing three good things and
     what caused them, daily, for one week). If the point is one
     meaningful moment, do the specific gratitude letter/visit. If the
     point is durable change, Three Good Things is the better-evidenced
     choice — ideally, do both.

8. **The closing frame.** Chasing status, resources, and certainty isn't
   a character flaw — those were the right targets for ancestral
   survival. They're miscalibrated targets for a modern marriage and a
   modern life. The update is available. It doesn't require new
   information so much as doing, consistently, what the evidence already
   says works.

# Why this belongs in Chapter 27 (The Discipline of Joy)

Chapter 27's premise is a man who has mastered steadiness but forgotten
to be glad, and its outline already calls for gratitude "as the
discipline of noticing what's actually here before it's gone." This
framework gives that chapter its evidence base: a real mechanism for why
achievement alone won't produce the gladness he's missing, and two
concrete, honestly-sourced practices rather than a vague permission to
"be more grateful." It also converges cleanly with the Stoic material
already planned for that chapter — Musonius Rufus's claim that what is
most excellent and what is most genuinely pleasant are the same thing is,
in effect, the ancient version of "achievement won't satisfy you, but the
practices that will are available now."

# Distinct from neighboring concepts

- **Not** [Marriage as Gratitude Under Impermanence](/frameworks/marriage-as-gratitude-under-impermanence.md)
  — that framework is the *memento mori* lens (gratitude because it's all
  going away). This framework is the psychological mechanism for *why*
  achievement fails to satisfy and *why* savoring/specific gratitude in
  particular work. The two pair well in prose: impermanence supplies the
  "why now," this supplies the "why this practice, specifically, and how."
- **Not** [The Virtue Question](/frameworks/the-virtue-question.md) — the
  Virtue Question is a real-time decision tool for a triggering moment.
  Savoring and specific gratitude are standing daily practices, run
  independent of any trigger.
