# Align on What You're Protecting, Not on the Rule

A rule is a position. Two positions in a marriage produce a standoff, and
standoffs get resolved by whoever cares more, holds out longer, or is more
tired. Nobody ever won "no screens after dinner."

What a rule protects is a shared interest, and shared interests are arguable in
a way positions are not. She may think he's wrong about the screens and agree
completely that their kids' attention is worth protecting right now. That is
two people working the same problem. The rule then follows from the agreement
instead of standing in for it.

The practical instruction: when the two of you disagree about a line, stop
arguing about the line. Ask what each of you thinks it's protecting, and have
that argument instead.

# Why this matters for Chapter 10

It converts the chapter's discernment test from a private exercise into a
marital one. Without it, "name what your boundary protects" is something a man
does alone in his own head, which is useful but small. With it, the same act is
what makes a boundary jointly held rather than personally enforced, and jointly
held boundaries are the only kind that survive a spouse who isn't convinced.

It also quietly answers an objection the chapter would otherwise leave open: a
reader could hear "decide your lines in advance and hold them" as a man
unilaterally imposing rules on his household. This framework is the difference.
He decides what he will and won't do; what the family protects is negotiated,
out loud, with his wife.
