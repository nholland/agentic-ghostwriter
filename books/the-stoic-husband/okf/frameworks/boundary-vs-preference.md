# Boundary vs. Preference — Protecting a Purpose, Not a Want

A preference says "I want this." A boundary protects something specific —
respect for his wife, the family's financial future, his kids' physical
safety, the marriage's actual stability. The test isn't how strongly he
feels about the rule. It's whether he can say, in one sentence, what the
rule is *for*. If he can't, it's a preference wearing a boundary's
clothes — and preferences don't hold up once someone pushes back, because
there's nothing underneath them to defend.

The author's own boundaries all pass this test cleanly: he doesn't let
friends run down his wife because he's protecting her standing, not his
own comfort. He and his wife don't spend past an agreed threshold without
talking first because they're protecting a shared future, not enforcing a
rule for its own sake. The family's one-time-loan policy protects the
family's own stability against a real, repeated pattern of non-repayment —
not stinginess.

# Why this matters for Chapter 10

This is the sharpened version of the chapter's Wisdom/discernment test (key
point 1 in `03-outline.md`). The outline's version asks
"does this person's influence make me more contemptuous, more willing to
lie, more likely to treat the marriage as a burden" — a good test, but one
that requires noticing a slow drift already underway. This framework gives
the reader a faster, first-pass gut-check he can run *before* the drift
starts: can I actually name what this boundary is protecting? Most men
enforce rules — with friends, with in-laws, with their kids — without ever
having done this work, which is exactly why the rule folds the first time
someone pushes back hard enough.

The author replied “Approved” to inbox 099's two prepared Chapter 10 passages.
Maintaining his own conduct means her crossing a line does not authorize his
crossing it. It does not require accepting repeated violations or remaining in
harm's way. The approved response ends an insulting conversation with a stated
return, returns when safe, names repetition and seeks help, and prioritizes
getting to safety if he is afraid or someone gets physical.

The list protects trust that can take years to rebuild. An apology matters,
and so does subsequent conduct. This replaces the chapter's absolute claim
that the protected part of a marriage does not repair. It neither guarantees
repair nor decides when someone should leave a marriage.
