# Double Standards — Which Ones Are Real

## Case 1: The same problem wearing two shapes

One of you shops small and often. The other buys big and rarely. Each looks at
the other and sees recklessness. It reads as a double standard and it is not
one — it is one problem, spending, showing up in two temperaments. A man who
raises this as unfairness has misread it, and she will know he has misread it
within one sentence.

## Case 2: A real difference with a real basis

The author's example, and it is the sharpest one: a mother can be upset and
raise her voice at the kids. When the father does it, it is frightening.

That is genuinely asymmetric, and part of the asymmetry is *true* — his volume
and size land differently in a child's body, and pretending otherwise helps
nobody. So this is a double standard that may be entirely legitimate.

What is *not* legitimate is that nobody ever said it out loud. The standard
gets enforced without ever having been discussed, which means he is being held
to a rule he never got to agree to, and cannot ask about without sounding like
he is asking for permission to yell.

**This is the best case in the chapter** precisely because it is ambiguous. The
reader cannot resolve it by deciding who is right. He can only resolve it by
doing the thing the chapter is teaching: saying it out loud, kindly, without
requiring her to concede.

## Case 3: A standard with nothing underneath it

She can be short with you when she is tired. When you are short with her, it
is a problem. She can raise something from three years ago. When you do, you
are keeping score.

One rule for her, one for you, and no basis for the difference that either of
you has ever named.

# Why This Is Conduct, Not Contribution

**This must not drift into who-does-more.** That is Ch7 (the ledger) and Ch8
(felt unfairness), both already written, and both about *contribution*. This is
about **conduct**: the standard each person is held to for how they behave, not
for how much they carry.

The distinction also supplies the chapter's sharpest move. A man sitting on a
conduct double standard has a philosophical excuse ready to hand: *Chapter 7
told me not to keep score.* He has borrowed a virtue from an earlier chapter to
license his silence. The answer is that not keeping score is about
contributions. Declining to name a conduct standard is not restraint, it is a
true thing he has decided not to say.
