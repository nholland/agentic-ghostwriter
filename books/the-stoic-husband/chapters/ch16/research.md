### B1. Opening: the capstone (~220 words), key point 1

**Rules that bind the opening.** Open in the reader's experience (`01-voice.md` Always Do), 1-3
sentences of orientation before anything else, never the lesson first, no quote or definition, not
"after a fight". **Do not open on a statement about warmth**: Ch15 opens "Warmth is easy on a good
day." Do not use the word "flow" here (its first use belongs to B5, see H1).

**Move.** Drop the reader into a good stretch he will recognize: an evening when nothing needs
managing, nobody is bracing, and the house is easy. This is a composite of the reader, not a scene
from the author's life (he gave none for the opening; do not invent one). Orientation: that ease is
what the whole book has been building toward. Then name what got him here, one clause each, using
the book's own words for each arc (see G2 for why these and not "storms"):
- **River, once:** calm when things change. The book's printed forms: Introduction "Calm and
  Adaptable", "the adaptability of the river"; Ch5 "keep flowing when the rocks hit" (do not reuse
  "flowing" here, H1).
- **Oak, once:** strength to carry weight. Introduction "Strong and Dependable", "He becomes
  load-bearing"; Ch5 "carry the weight over time". **The storm is the Oak's** (Introduction: "Its
  roots go deeper than the storm"; Part II page). The outline's paraphrase "calm got him through the
  storms" gives the River the Oak's weather; write calm with change or rocks, strength with weight.
- **Then warmth as what those were for.** The author's frame (Round 6): "The love that you're giving,

### B5. Flow, and the three words (~480 words), key point 5

Sources: `okf/frameworks/marriage-in-flow.md`, `okf/frameworks/equanimity-arete-eudaimonia.md`,
`okf/citations/csikszentmihalyi-1990-flow.md`, `okf/citations/diogenes-laertius-7-87-90-eudaimonia-and-arete.md`.

1. **Name it, his words (Round 6):** "there's a larger concept that is popular in business that people
   call flow." / "Flow is effectively when everything is going well: you're at peace and you're
   operating at your highest self."
2. **Credit, one sentence, paraphrase, no numbers, no quotation:** the psychologist Mihaly
   Csikszentmihalyi named flow in *Flow: The Psychology of Optimal Experience* (1990): the state of
   being so absorbed in something that stretches you, without overwhelming you, that you stop
   watching yourself and the doing becomes its own reward. Say his name plainly once; the audiobook
   reader will need it, which is a production note, not prose.
3. **Say the step is his, in one sentence.** Csikszentmihalyi's flow is one person's experience of an
   activity. Treating a marriage as one thing that can be in flow, and the three Stoic words as what
   flow looks like for it, is the author's step (his: "Again the three Stoic words are representative
   of flow."). **Do not write that Csikszentmihalyi never applied flow to relationships**: summaries
   report a section on flow and the family (unconfirmed). And **"at peace" is the author's emphasis**:
   Csikszentmihalyi's flow is engaged and stretched, not restful. Give the definition as "the way I
   use it", not as Csikszentmihalyi's.
4. **Handle the River's word.** See H1; default: one sentence acknowledging that the book has used
   "flow" for the River's calm through change, and that here it is the whole marriage.
5. **The three words, each glossed at first use, concept before word, no brackets:**
   - **Equanimity** (callback; Ch1 85 and Ch2 60 defined it, "staying calm and steady on the inside"):
     his "when you are in equanimity as an individual, you are at peace"; the marriage at peace.
   - **Arête** (accent; first printed use in the book): Greek for excellence; for the Stoics,
     excellence of character. His reading, as his: "at your highest self" / "highest potential"
     (`02-audience.md` line 71 uses the same "highest potential" reading). The marriage at its best.
     Do not pose "the Arête question" (reserved for the Introduction's later echoes, Ch18, Ch25, Ch27,
     Conclusion: `okf/frameworks/arete-as-secular-load-bearer.md`).
   - **Eudaimonia** (picked up from B3): happiness or flourishing; a life going well as a whole, not a
     passing mood. The marriage flourishing.
   Source for the glosses: DL VII.87-90 (paraphrase only; Hicks is not a house edition).
   Optional bridge, **author's call (H2)**: the early Stoics described the happy life as a good, smooth
   flow of life (DL VII.88). Out unless he takes it.
6. **Us over me, his words (required):** "Being happy as an individual is extremely different than
   being happy in a relationship." and "the health of the whole is more important than the health of
   the individual" (outline quotes the second; use both or the second alone).
7. **The ah-ha, verbatim, as narration (no quotation marks), at the end of B5:**
   A marriage is one thing the two of you tend together. Keep it at peace, at its best, and
   flourishing, and it moves into flow.
   It closes B5. Nothing after it in the section restates it. It is the Publisher's wording, approved
   by him; do not attribute it to him as something he said.

**Image control in B5:** no current, stream, river-flowing, smooth water, garden, growing roots.
"Flow" itself is the concept's name, not an image; keep it to about five uses in the chapter
(name, his definition, Csikszentmihalyi, the River sentence, the ah-ha).

## D. Joint-account fences (Ch7, Ch12, the Prologue, Ch24)

Proposed as one concept update, `runs/ch16/proposed-concepts.md` P1 (open). The brief applies them
now; if he edits P1, D follows his edit.

1. **One account, nobody counting.** Ch7 (line 33): a tally that reads only your side is "arithmetic
   done on one page of a two-page ledger". The joint account has one page. Never "keep the count",
   never "what have you put in over the last few weeks", never his deposits tallied, listed against
   time, or remembered for later. Ch7 line 15 already used "the way you know your bank balance
   without checking it" for the tally: the account is never checked to see who is ahead.
2. **Reciprocation is what a working account looks like, never a condition.** The Prologue (45):
   "invest in it on your own initiative, regardless of what she's doing with her half". He puts in
   whether or not she does. Her deposits: one plain sentence, not listed, not compared.
3. **Her patience is never a return on his deposits** (v1 panel 1, coherence E1; Ch8 55 and Ch15 59).

## H. Open questions only the author can answer

None blocks drafting; each has a default the Ghostwriter follows until he rules.

- **H1. "Flow" and the River.** The book has used "flow" for the River since the premise ("When life
  changes, flow."). The capstone uses it for all three together. **Default:** one sentence in B5

- **H2. The Stoics' own "flow of life".** Diogenes Laertius VII.88 records the early Stoic phrase for

  partly the Stoics' idea, not only his step; it adds a river image and a non-house translation
