## Claims That Need Support

### Claim 1: The psychology of scorekeeping — it starts as a fairness mechanism and becomes a grievance engine
**Best evidence type:** Author-synthesized framework + a calibration note so the chapter doesn't assume every reader is a scorekeeper.
**What to look for:** Not external research — this is a named pattern, not a statistical claim.
**Strongest counterargument:** "Tracking fairness is reasonable — someone has to notice if the division of labor is lopsided." The chapter should concede this: noticing imbalance isn't the problem (that's Ch8's honest-imbalance territory); converting it into a private tally used as ammunition is.
**Status:** **Backed by an existing concept** — see `/okf/frameworks/scorekeeping-is-contagious.md` (the fairness-mechanism-to-grievance-engine arc, with the schema: felt injustice → named or shown → received as attack → counter-ledger opened → two parallel audits) and `/okf/frameworks/scorekeeping-varies-by-marriage.md` (the calibration note — scorekeeping is a dial, not universal; some marriages run it constantly, others barely register it at all). Use the calibration note early so a low-scorekeeping reader doesn't feel mis-diagnosed.

**Scope decision (2026-06-28):** Scorekeeping turns out to have at least six
versions — task, impact/value, sacrifice, competence, grievance, and intimacy
— captured in the new framework `/okf/frameworks/six-ledgers-of-scorekeeping.md`.
A reader who's never tallied chores might still run the impact ledger ("my
income is the real contribution") or the competence ledger ("she's better
with the kids, I'm better with money") without ever calling it scorekeeping.
**Decision for Ch7: name the wider pattern briefly, early in the chapter** (a
sentence or two acknowledging this isn't just about dishes — it shows up as
whose work counts more, who sacrificed more, who's better at what, who's
wronged whom, who wanted whom more) so more readers see themselves in the
diagnosis, **then go deep on the task ledger only** (small rocks/big rocks)
as the chapter's one fully-developed illustration, per its outline slot and
word count target. Do not attempt to fully develop all six ledgers here —
that would break the one-metaphor and word-count rules. The other ledgers
get full development elsewhere: impact/value and competence in Ch8 (honest
imbalance), intimacy in the Part III sex chapter, grievance as a callback to
Ch6/Part I contempt material.

### Claim 2: Why the ledger can't balance — they're not playing the same game; each side counts different categories with different weights
**Best evidence type:** Concrete mental model (primary) + external validation (secondary).
**What to look for:** A clean schema distinguishing visible/repeatable contributions from invisible/cognitive ones, plus an outside source confirming that scorekeeping itself (regardless of who's "ahead") is bad practice in durable marriages.
**Strongest counterargument:** "If I don't track it, nothing changes — score-keeping is how I advocate for myself." The chapter's answer: the structural mismatch (Claim 2) is *why* tracking doesn't work as an advocacy tool, not just why it feels bad — and Claim 3 (jurisdiction) is the actual alternative to either tracking or giving up.
**Status:**
- **Backed by an existing concept:** `/okf/frameworks/small-rocks-big-rocks.md` — the core mental model: "small rocks" (visible, repeatable tasks — dishes, laundry, school runs) vs. "big rocks" (invisible cognitive load — planning, anticipating, managing open loops). Both ledgers can be honest and still produce opposite verdicts because they're auditing different books. This is the chapter's central image for Claim 2.
- **Backed by an existing concept:** `/okf/citations/pillemer-dont-keep-score.md` — Karl Pillemer's Cornell Marriage Advice Project (700+ long-married elders, marriages averaging 44 years) found the successful framing is "give more than you get," not 50/50 accounting. Status: **verifiable** — real source identified, but exact quotes/page numbers still need verbatim confirmation (Cornell Chronicle article returns 403; ScienceDaily mirror available). Do not present as a verbatim quote until refine-stage verification confirms wording. **Do NOT attribute "don't sweat the small stuff" to Pillemer** — that's Richard Carlson, a different source entirely; the citation file flags this explicitly as a known mix-up risk.
- **Backed by a new concept (added 2026-06-28):** `/okf/citations/park-et-al-2025-pay-me-back-exchange-orientation.md` — a 13-year longitudinal study of 7,293 German couples (Park, Johnson, Gordon & Impett, *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin*, 2025) found that "exchange orientation" (expecting direct reciprocation for what you give a partner) predicts declining satisfaction over time; most people naturally relax this as bonds deepen, but those who stay transactional see satisfaction erode faster. Status: **verifiable**. This is a stronger empirical anchor than Pillemer alone — Pillemer is attributed elder wisdom, this is a large-N peer-reviewed study of the exact mechanism. Use both: Pillemer for the warm, quotable "give more than you get" framing; Park et al. for the "here's what the data actually shows happens to couples who don't" backing.
- **Resolved (was RESEARCH NEEDED, now backed — added 2026-06-28):** `/okf/citations/gillespie-peterson-lever-2019-fairness-housework-expenses.md` — closes the gap previously tracked at `/okf/citations/household-labor-and-caretaker-burden.md`. A study of 10,236 couples (PLOS ONE, 2019) found that feeling personally treated unfairly predicts relationship quality more than perceiving your partner as unfairly treated, and that fairness over shared *expenses* predicts relationship quality more than fairness over *housework*. Status: **verifiable**. Uses plain "fairness" language, not "emotional labor"/"mental load" — fits `02-audience.md`'s register rules. The `household-labor-and-caretaker-burden.md` citation file itself remains `unverified` pending author review of whether to formally supersede it with this new citation.
- **[RESEARCH NEEDED: confirm the Sisyphean-labor study the author is recalling]** — see `/okf/citations/sisyphean-labor-and-meaning.md`. Status: unverified. The author recalled a study where participants were paid escalating amounts to do repetitive manual work, and a researcher periodically destroyed the completed work and had them redo it — motivation collapsed once the work was revealed as pointless. Best guess is Dan Ariely's "Sisyphean condition" experiments (Lego/Bionicle assembly, described in *The Upside of Irrationality* and a widely-cited TED talk), but this is unconfirmed. If verified in time, this strengthens the "big rocks never end" texture — household labor isn't just unevenly distributed, some of it is structurally Sisyphean (laundry, dishes) regardless of who does it. Optional for Ch7; do not block the draft on this — it's a texture enhancer, not the load-bearing claim.

### Claim 3: Jurisdiction — the ledger is an attempt to govern someone he doesn't govern; the only column he controls is his own
**Best evidence type:** Stoic philosophical anchor (primary) — this is the chapter's resolution, not just a claim, so it needs to land as the payoff of Claims 1 and 2.
**What to look for:** Direct connection to the dichotomy of control already established in Chapters 1–3, applied specifically to the scoreboard.
**Strongest counterargument:** "This sounds like 'just let it go and stop expecting fairness' — i.e., capitulation dressed as wisdom." This exact objection is flagged in `02-audience.md` ("This puts all the responsibility on me. What about her?"). The chapter must distinguish *governing only your column* from *accepting unfairness silently* — that distinction is what Chapter 8 exists to develop further; Ch7 should plant the seed ("sometimes the imbalance is real — more on that next chapter") without resolving it here.
**Status:** **Backed by an existing concept** — see `/okf/frameworks/dichotomy-of-control-in-marriage.md` (the schema of what's "not yours" vs. "yours" in conflict, collapsing to the core reframe: *What kind of spouse am I becoming in response?*) and `/okf/frameworks/the-role-frame-ego-vs-role.md` (ego asks "Am I winning?" / "Did they admit I was right?"; role asks "What does love require?" / "What is mine to repair?" — the scorekeeping mind is pure ego-frame; the jurisdiction move is the shift to role-frame).
- **Backed by a new concept (added 2026-06-28):** `/okf/citations/seneca-de-beneficiis-against-keeping-accounts.md` — Seneca's *De Beneficiis*, the only complete surviving ancient treatise on gift exchange, teaches directly against keeping a ledger of benefits given: a gift given "out at interest" (with an expectation of return) stops functioning as a gift. This is the most specific classical anchor for the chapter's central vice (transactional giving), distinct from the dichotomy-of-control frameworks, which describe jurisdiction's *structure* rather than the *vice* scorekeeping represents. Status: **verifiable** — real treatise, well-documented central teaching, but exact book/chapter location and a verbatim quote in a citable translation are not yet pinned down (web access to primary text blocked during this research pass); confirm before manuscript use.
- **Backed by a new concept (added 2026-06-28):** `/okf/citations/marcus-aurelius-meditations-book7-no-repayment.md` — a Meditations Book 7 passage, commonly rendered as "When you have done a good act and another has received it, why do you still look for a third thing besides — credit for having done good, or a return for it?" A concise, single-line companion to the longer Seneca material — useful as a closing or transition beat once the ledger argument has been made at length. Status: **verifiable** — passage and substance well attested, but exact verse numbering varies by translation/edition and has not been confirmed against the author's physical copy (per Rule 11).

**What makes a good story here:** A scene where two genuine, non-performed grievances collide over something small (the outline specifies laundry/dishes-adjacent), and the reader sees both ledgers are honest and still incompatible.
**Status:** **Backed by an existing concept** — see `/okf/stories/two-truths-tired-husband-overloaded-wife.md`. Composite, author-disclosed. Concrete texture: he's done the dishes, put the kids to bed, sat down; she comes in with a laundry basket late (he's asked her not to do this before); the room goes quiet; he can help (feels like enabling a pattern he's asked her to change) or not help (is seen as the guy on the couch). ## Analogies and Mental Models

1. **Two honest auditors, two different sets of books:** "You're both keeping an honest ledger. You're just not auditing the same company." Works because it validates both people's honesty while explaining the irreconcilability structurally rather than morally. Risk: "auditing" language could read as cold/corporate if overused — use once as the anchor image, then drop into plainer language (small rocks/big rocks) for the rest of the chapter.
2. **A scoreboard with no shared rulebook:** Two teams on the same field, each scoring points by their own rules — one team gets a point every time a dish gets washed, the other every time a problem gets silently prevented before it happens. Works because it's visual and concrete for a reader who thinks in systems (per `02-audience.md`, this reader thinks in habits and systems). Risk: sports framing can feel like it's trivializing a real source of resentment — keep it brief, pair immediately with the emotional stakes (the "I knew you wouldn't raise a finger" line).

## Structural Note

This chapter is the direct continuation of Chapter 6's resentment thread (the dishes story) — Ch6 ended on "resentment isn't about the dishes, it's about why I've been doing them"; Ch7 should open assuming the reader already understands resentment-from-performed-duty and now turn the lens onto the *measuring* behavior that resentment produces. This also pre-empts the `02-audience.md` objection "This puts all the responsibility on me" — Ch7's jurisdiction move is about *how he keeps score*, not a claim that her behavior never matters.
