# Voice Constitution
*The author's voice spec for this book. Read this before writing any prose.*

---

## Voice in Five Words
**Story-first, concrete, tension-honest, direct, grounded.**

---

## Sentence Rhythm

Sentence length follows what the moment needs, not a pattern. Sometimes a short sentence lands a point. Sometimes a longer one needs room to build a thought, and that's where it should sit. It doesn't owe the reader a punchline afterward. A paragraph doesn't have to resolve into a short line to have done its job. Often it just continues, and the next paragraph picks up the thread. Contractions always. No "do not" unless emphasis demands it.

Emphasis comes from directness, not adverbs. When something is bad, say it's bad. When it hit you, say it hit you. Hedge words that soften an emotional truth (*he was kind of upset*) are a voice drift warning. Naming who a claim applies to (*if this sounds familiar, for a lot of men*) is different — that's honesty about the claim's reach, not hedging.

**Target rhythm example:**
> He didn't check his phone. He didn't raise his voice. He didn't storm out or shut down or go cold. She said her piece, and he just sat there with it, like a man who'd already decided he wasn't going to let this moment make him into someone smaller. That's not passivity. That's the hardest thing a man can do.

**Long-sentence cap (counted, added 2026-07-23 after a cross-chapter comparison found Ch8 running at 22% long sentences against a Ch1-7 range of 1-15%, avg 5%).** No more than roughly 10% of a chapter's sentences should run 25 words or longer. This isn't a ban on long sentences; a chapter can and should stretch one out when a thought needs the room. It's a cap on how often that happens. A chapter that leans on long, multi-clause sentences as its default rhythm rather than an occasional move reads denser and more argumentative than this book's baseline, even when every individual sentence is fine on its own. Verify by literal count during refine, not by impression, per "Verification, Not Impression" below.

## Always Do

- **Open in the reader's experience.** Every chapter opens from inside the reader's head — not by watching a scene unfold, but by dropping into a feeling, a moment of recognition, or a direct question the reader is already carrying. The reader is the protagonist. You are talking to him, not narrating a couple from the outside. "We've all been there — your wife says something, and you feel it before you've even processed what it meant." That's the entry point. Not: "He sat across from her. The room was quiet."
- **Keep direct address the chapter's default gear, not an occasional return to it.** (Counted, added 2026-07-23 after a cross-chapter comparison found Ch8 running "you/your" at 28 per 1,000 words against a Ch1-7 range of 37-62, avg 52.) A chapter can and should step back to name a mechanism in general terms, but it should keep returning to "you," not treat direct address as bookends around a middle section that talks about the pattern instead of to the reader. Target roughly 40+ instances of "you/your" per 1,000 words. Verify by literal count during refine, not by impression.
- **Name the concept.** If the chapter introduces a new idea, give it a handle. The name is what the reader carries home. The story is the anchor.
- **Let someone else's line land the punchline.** When the chapter turns, the climax belongs to Marcus Aurelius, Epictetus, Seneca — or to a line from a real exchange in a marriage. Use the quote that actually fits and lands, regardless of how well-known it is. The test is relevance, not rarity.
- **Stage your own mistakes in real time.** Put the reader inside the version of you that was about to get it wrong. Not "I used to think X" — show the moment before you knew better.
- **Frame the tension, don't resolve it.** Marriage is almost always both things at once. Name what's pulling in each direction. Trust the reader to live in that.
- **Use concrete, physical metaphors.** One per chapter. The Stoics taught with the simplest possible images — the archer, the river, the wax tablet. Find the one image that anchors the chapter and return to it. **Cap the return:** count every word in the metaphor's family (e.g., for a machine/program image: *operating system, software, code, installed, hardware, program, run, default*) as one mention each. A piece of any length gets roughly 3 total mentions of that family, not one per sentence or paragraph. Past that, the image stops being an anchor and becomes the only available vocabulary — flag it as overuse, the same as stacking two different metaphors. This applies harder in short-form pieces (Substack, social) than in full chapters: the same absolute count of repetitions reads far denser in 500 words than in 2,500.
- **Use specific, named detail.** Not "a husband." A moment. Not "she was upset." What she said, what he did, the exact shape of the thing.
- **Orient before the scene.** Every chapter opens with 1–3 sentences of orientation before the first story or scene drops. Not a lesson — one sentence that tells the reader what he is about to feel or realize. A compass, not a curtain-raiser. The orientation is the reader's entry point into his own experience, not a description of external circumstances.
- **Explain every Stoic concept in plain English on first use.** When any Greek term, Latin phrase, or named Stoic concept is introduced, a plain-language explanation follows immediately — same sentence or the next. The target reader has heard of Stoicism but hasn't studied it. Write as if the concept has never been named before. For larger concepts (an idea that takes a paragraph or more to land), go further: describe the pattern in plain language first, using the reader's own experience, and only attach the Stoic term afterward as a label for what he just recognized. Term-first, explanation-second inverts the order the reader needs.
- **Ground classical figures in plain context when their circumstances matter.** If the point depends on knowing who Marcus Aurelius, Seneca, or Epictetus was, or what situation they were in (an emperor at war, a man in exile, a former slave writing to his student), say so in plain words at first substantial use. Phrases like "on campaign" or "in exile" assume the reader already has the biography. A reader who knows it skims past the reminder in half a second; a reader who doesn't needs it for the reference to land at all.
- **Default to accumulated friction over discrete fights.** The lived texture of marriage tension is small moments: a tone of voice, an offhand comment, the look across the kitchen, something left unsaid. Write to that 80%. Reserve fight-framing for chapters specifically about fighting (Ch1, Ch5). Elsewhere, the trigger is almost always smaller. An opening that begins with "after a fight" or "lying in bed after an argument" will misrepresent what most readers carry into the chapter.
- **When a chapter addresses a pattern the reader may recognize in himself, lead with honest acknowledgment before diagnosis.** Chapters on negative patterns (anger, withdrawal, avoidance, scorekeeping) must earn the right to name the problem by first naming why the problem makes sense — including why it worked, why it was inherited, and why it persists. The reader should feel understood before he feels challenged. The tone is a doctor explaining a mechanism, not a judge delivering a verdict.

## Never Do

- **Never open with the lesson.** No "In this chapter, we'll explore..." No abstract statement of the principle before the scene has landed. The reader must be inside the moment before he gets the framework.
- **Never wrap up with a moral.** No "And the lesson here is..." The story earns its own meaning. Close on the chapter's own image, in the author's voice — not on someone else's line. The Stoic quote does its work earlier, where it turns the chapter; the final words land the chapter's idea in an image the author could have arrived at himself, then stop. (House standard, established Ch2–5: the close is the author's own sentence, not a quote followed by an echo.)
- **Never write two endings.** The close is exactly one sentence. The sentence that follows it may not restate, explain, or qualify what the first one just landed ("You can't rewrite a program you've never admitted exists. Seeing it clearly is the part most men skip..." — the second sentence drains the first). If you've written a strong closing line and the urge is to add a sentence that unpacks it, that's the signal you're not done trusting the line. Cut the second sentence.
- **Never let bold text do a sentence's job.** Bold is typographic emphasis standing in for a sentence that should already land on its own construction. If a sentence needs bold to register, rewrite the sentence until it doesn't, then remove the bold. This applies to short-form pieces too — a single bolded sentence used as a genuine pull-quote (one per piece, at most) is the only exception, and even that should be earned, not routine. Bolded **run-in section headers** are structure, not emphasis, and fall outside this cap: a short label — eight words at most, closing with a period or a colon — that opens a section, whether set on its own line (`**The Gap**`) or leading its own paragraph (`**The fix.** The internet goes down on a Tuesday night...`). The cap of one still governs every other bolded span, including a bolded sentence used as emphasis mid-paragraph.
- **Never let one rhetorical device carry the whole piece.** A reframe ("That's not X. That's Y."), a negated comparison ("you don't do X, but you're still doing X-adjacent"), a colon-then-label sentence, or any other single sentence-shape is a strong move once, sometimes twice. By the third repetition the reader has spotted the gear turning and the device stops working — predictability replaces surprise. Vary the construction: a direct statement, a question, a story beat, a flat assertion. No single device repeats more than twice in one piece, chapter or short-form.
- **Never stack metaphors.** One concrete image per chapter, max. Pick it and commit. If the anchor metaphor is working, don't also bring in three other images.
- **Never use corporate or therapy-speak.** *Leverage, synergize, process your feelings, unpack, journey, toxic.* If the word would appear in a couples counseling brochure or a management deck, reach for something else.
- **Never write performative vulnerability.** Self-aware in passing, not confessional. A sentence about being wrong, not a paragraph about your feelings.
- **Never treat Stoicism as armor.** The Stoic Husband is not cold, invulnerable, or above emotion. He is deeply present and internally grounded. The book earns warmth; it never avoids it.
- **Never write "As a husband, you must..."** Directive without earning the directive is lecturing. Show the moment first. The directive arrives after the reader has already felt it.
- **Never describe a scene from the outside.** No "She came through the door looking distracted." No "He paused before answering." No composite vignette that puts the reader in the audience. If there's a scene in this book, the reader IS the man in it — experiencing it, not observing it. Third-person scene-setting is a drama move, not a guide move.
- **Never use windup sentences.** A windup sentence announces what the next sentence will say instead of just saying it. "Here's what she doesn't say in the moment." "There's something worth naming here." "Here's what this looks like in practice." These sentences add no content. They create a dramatic pause before a reveal that doesn't need one. Cut them. Start with the substance.

- **Never make the reader infer a thing you could name, and never write for cleverness.** The test for every sentence is: does this help the reader? Not: does this sound smart? If a sentence serves the author's voice more than the reader's clarity, simplify it or cut it. We are not writing for a Pulitzer. We are writing to help men. The most common form this takes is not fancy vocabulary, it's describing a plain concept indirectly instead of naming it: *"You have a number in your head above which you'd want to talk before the money left the account"* is four clauses for *"you have boundaries on money."* *"Saying a line out loud isn't unkind"* is *"setting a boundary isn't unkind."* The indirect version feels more literary and reads as evasion, because the reader has to do a small piece of work the sentence should have done. **Test:** can the clause be replaced with the plain noun? Then replace it. This is related to the windup rule but distinct: a windup announces what the next sentence will say, this one gestures at a thing instead of naming it. **The same instinct, applied to argument: never answer an objection the reader hasn't raised.** *"Boundaries aren't distance from the people you love"* plants a doubt that wasn't there and then defeats it. Argue against what the reader actually believes, per `02-audience.md`'s "What the Reader Already Believes," not against a position invented to be knocked down. **The third form is negative social proof: never establish the value of something by pointing at the men who missed it.** *"This is what the work was for, and a lot of men walk straight past it"* invents a crowd to fail so the reader can feel like the exception. It gives him nothing to do, and the flattery is the only thing it delivers. Say the thing to him directly instead: *"Don't spend this one waiting for the next one."* All three are the same move, inventing a foil rather than arguing with what is actually in the reader's head. Added 2026-08-23, after the author caught it on the Part V page and asked for the technique removed by name.
- **Never use em-dashes.** Em-dashes have become a fingerprint of AI-generated prose. Readers have a visceral reaction against them. Use a period, colon, or comma instead. Each forces a more precise decision about how ideas connect: a period breaks, a colon elaborates, a comma continues. When you feel the pull toward an em-dash, that's a signal to decide what the sentence is actually doing and punctuate accordingly. **Exception:** inside a direct quotation from a published Stoic translation, keep the translator's own punctuation, including em-dashes, exactly as published. This only applies once the wording (and the em-dash) has been confirmed against the source, recorded in that citation's `okf/citations/{slug}.md` concept — its `verification_note` must say explicitly that the em-dash is the translator's own punctuation, not house style. `citation-queue.md` lists which citations carry that confirmation. — if unconfirmed, punctuate the quote as if it were original prose.
- **Never use "move/moves/moved" as a default verb for how things travel or transfer.** It has become a fingerprint of AI-generated prose — overused to the point where it registers as mechanical rather than intentional. Replace with the specific verb the moment actually calls for: *travels, runs, lands, arrives, passes, spreads, transfers, shifts, enters, carries*. The word can appear once if nothing else fits, but it should never appear more than once in a single chapter or post, and never as the go-to verb for emotional or physical transfer.

- **Never frame a behavioral pattern as a moral failure.** "You have an anger problem" is a verdict. "Anger makes sense — here's what it actually is and why it persists" is a diagnosis. This book is written for men who are trying to improve, not men being convicted. The distinction applies sentence by sentence in every chapter that names a pattern the reader might recognize in himself.
- **Never cast the wife's mood, reaction, or behavior as a threat to defend against.** The dichotomy of control ("her mood isn't yours to govern") is a fact about where his attention belongs, not a warning about danger arriving from her direction. Words like *undefended, infected, absorb* (used as invasion), or *defense* applied to her turn a neutral boundary into a siege — she becomes the opposing force and he becomes a man under attack, which is the opposite of what these chapters actually argue. State what's outside his control as fact, not danger: "her mood is hers, not a signal to read as a threat," not "the man who doesn't know this is undefended." Test: if the sentence would work aimed at a stranger you're worried will hurt you, it's using the wrong frame for a marriage.
- **Never re-use the "not a poster" construction to introduce Marcus Aurelius quotes.** Chapter 1 uses "That's not a poster. That's a man..." authentically — it is the author's own line. Using the same framing in subsequent chapter drafts is AI pattern-matching, not voice. Each Marcus Aurelius quote needs its own specific contextual introduction, not this recycled frame.
- **Never write dialogue or internal narration using absolutist framing ("every single time," "always," "never," as a dramatized accusation).** Real dialogue inside a marriage rarely holds up to "every single time" — and a reader who's actually lived the moment being described notices the exaggeration and discounts the whole passage. If a scene needs the *feeling* of a repeated pattern, name the pattern directly ("this keeps happening" or "you can see it coming before she says a word") rather than reaching for a dramatized absolute. Reserve "always"/"never" for the rare case where the author is naming his own retrospective realization about a pattern, not putting the word in a character's mouth mid-scene.
- **Never assert a universal claim about all men ("every man is built this way," "all men do this," "no man wants that").** This is the same overclaim problem as the dialogue rule above, but in the narrator's own voice describing a pattern rather than in a character's mouth mid-scene — a distinct failure, not a duplicate of it. A flat universal is one counterexample away from a reader dismissing the whole claim, and plenty of readers will supply that counterexample from their own life. The line already distinguishes claim-scope honesty from hedging (see Sentence Rhythm, above: "for a lot of men" names reach, it doesn't soften a truth) — this rule is the other half of that same instinct, applied to the size of the claim rather than its emotional temperature. Use "many men," "most men," "a lot of men," or "the man who [does X]" instead. The goal is a wide net, not a universal law — inclusive without becoming a claim precise enough for one reader to falsify by being the exception.

## Channel and Artifact Rules

Every artifact produced for this book — a chapter, a Substack post, a social
teaser — is a **standalone object written for a specific reader in a specific
context**. The reader of each artifact holds only that artifact. They have not
read the others. Write accordingly.

**The Substack reader** is reading an email or a web post. They don't have the
book. They may never read the book. The post must be worth their time entirely
on its own. It does not exist to sell the book. It does not point to the book.
It does not say "for more, read..." or "[Book Title] picks up from here..." or
"in the chapters ahead..." or anything that frames the post as a preview of
something better. If the post is good, the reader will want more. That desire
is earned by the quality of what they just read, not engineered by explicit
pointers at the end.

**The social reader** is reading three sentences, probably on a phone. They
haven't seen the Substack post. The teaser earns the click by making the reader
feel they're missing something worth having — not by summarizing what they'll
find or advertising a longer piece. It ends with a link, not a pitch.

**The general rule:** no artifact references another artifact as something the
reader should go consume. The reader's path from social → Substack → book is
a natural consequence of quality at each step, not a chain of explicit
cross-references you have to construct. Each layer closes cleanly and stops.

**Banned in Substack posts, regardless of phrasing:**
"this book," "the rest of this book," "this chapter," "[Title] picks up from
here," "[Title] goes deeper into this," "in the chapters ahead," "spends the
next N pages on," or any construction that points the reader toward content
they don't yet have.

## Verification, Not Impression

Every rule above falls into one of two kinds, and they get checked differently.

**Qualitative rules** (corporate jargon, performative vulnerability, scene-from-outside, windup sentences) are judged by reading. A holistic pass catches these — they're about register and feel, and "does this sound right" is a valid test.

**Counted rules** are anything with a number or a structural threshold attached: the bold cap (at most one per piece, run-in section headers excluded), the metaphor word-family cap (~3 mentions per 1,000 words, scaled by length; up to 5 where the anchor image carries the chapter's mechanism rather than decorating it, declared in Draft Notes), the ending (exactly one sentence, nothing after it), the rhetorical-device cap (no single sentence-shape more than twice), the long-sentence cap (≤10% of sentences at 25+ words), and the direct-address floor ("you/your" at 40+ per 1,000 words). These cannot be judged by impression. A piece can read as fine on a re-read while still failing the count — repetition that doesn't register consciously while writing is exactly the failure mode these rules exist to catch.

**The rule:** after any revision claims to satisfy a counted rule, verify it by literally counting, in a separate pass from the one that did the rewriting:
- **Metaphor family:** count every word in the family, including verb conjugations and tense variants (run, running, runs all count; installed, installs, install all count). Estimate-by-feel routinely undercounts these by two or more.
- **Rhetorical device repetition:** count every instance of the device, including a mini-instance embedded inside a larger sentence ("installed, not chosen" inside another sentence counts as one instance of the negation-pair device, even though it's not its own sentence).
- **Bold and endings:** a direct text search, not a re-read — count the `**` markers, confirm nothing follows the final sentence.
- **Long-sentence percentage:** split the chapter into sentences, count words in each, and compute the share at 25+ words. Do this with an actual count (script or literal tally), not a read-through impression — sentence length is one of the easiest things to misjudge while inside the prose you just wrote.
- **Direct-address density:** count every "you/your/you've/you're" instance and divide by chapter word count, times 1,000. A chapter can feel second-person throughout while still testing low if a long middle stretch shifts into naming a pattern generally rather than addressing the reader.
- **Register shifts** (no clean count exists, but still falls outside the qualitative category): check verb mood directly. If the original violation used second-person imperative verbs, the fix fails if it still uses second-person imperative verbs, even different ones. Swapping "pick" for "trace" or "ask" for "find" is not a register change — it's the same instruction in different words. A real fix changes the sentence's grammatical mood (declarative, observational, a question) or its scope, not just its vocabulary.

Do not present a revision as compliant with a counted rule until it has been counted. "I cut it from ~9 to 3" is a claim; it is only true if the second number came from an actual count of the final text, not the writer's impression of how much was removed.

## Relationship with Evidence

Evidence is story first, principle late. The research — Stoic texts, Gottman's findings, recognizable composite scenarios from marriage — arrives after the reader is already inside a moment they recognize. Statistics, when used, get the "so what" immediately: not just the number, but what the number means to a man reading this in bed at 11pm wondering what's wrong with his marriage.

Composite scenarios are the primary evidence vehicle. They must feel lived, not constructed — specific enough that the reader thinks "that's me," not "imagine a husband and wife having a disagreement." The specific detail does the credibility work even when the character is composite.

Classical Stoic texts (Marcus Aurelius, Seneca, Epictetus, Musonius Rufus) are the philosophical anchor. Use the passage that fits and serves the reader — famous or not. The goal is relevance, not signal that you've read the obscure texts. Quote them in their actual voice, not paraphrased into neutrality. Let them land the climax where they can.

The author's own first-person moments are evidence of a specific kind: he's been inside this, made the central mistake (Stoicism as armor), and his wife helped him find the correct path. That experience shows up as evidence, not as biography.

## Relationship with Personal Narrative

First-person appears as evidence, not performance. He's in the book because he lived it — 23 years, every test, made the central mistake himself. He doesn't narrate from the position of having figured it out. He puts the reader inside the version of himself that was about to get humbled.

The self-deprecating marker is a sentence, not a paragraph. *"I was defensive, which is a little embarrassing."* The self-awareness is a tic, not a performance. Never a confession.

The author's story runs underneath the book like a current, not on top of it like a memoir. He surfaces at the moments where it matters — where his own mistake is the clearest example of what the chapter warns against. Then he steps back. The reader is the protagonist.

## Voice Transformation Examples
*These show the difference between generic prose and this author's voice. They illustrate tone and register. Don't treat how these examples end as a structural template — the shape of the closing line here is incidental, not the recipe.*

**Generic:**
> When men feel criticized by their partners, they often become defensive. This defensive response can escalate conflict and damage the relationship over time. Learning to respond differently requires practice and intentionality.

**This voice:**
> She said something sharp and you felt it in your chest — that familiar pull toward the door, or toward silence, or toward saying something back that you'd regret. That's the moment. Not the fight. Not the aftermath. That three-second window where everything you are decides whether you're going to lead or react. Most men react. They learned it from their fathers, who never had a name for it either.

---

**Generic:**
> Stoicism teaches us that we cannot control external events, only our responses to them. This principle is especially relevant in marriage, where we often encounter situations that challenge our equanimity.

**This voice:**
> Marcus Aurelius was a Roman emperor. For years he lived away from home, leading his army through wars he didn't choose, while losing people he loved. He wrote *Meditations* for himself, not for anyone else to read. *"You have power over your mind, not outside events."* That's not a poster. That's a man in a hard season, reminding himself of the one thing he still owned. He had it. You have it. The question is whether you're going to use it, or let her tone of voice make that decision for you.

---

**Generic:**
> It is important to understand that Stoicism is not about suppressing emotions or becoming cold and distant in your relationship.

**This voice:**
> Here's the mistake I made first. I read the books, learned about the dichotomy of control, and thought I was making progress. My wife thought I'd gone somewhere she couldn't reach. She wasn't wrong. I'd built something invulnerable and called it growth. That's not the Stoic Husband. That's just a new way of disappearing.

## Red Flags

- **Opening with the principle.** Any draft that begins with an abstract statement of what the chapter is about has slipped. Find the scene. Start there.
- **"As a husband, you should..."** This construction shows up when the writing is trying to deliver the lesson without earning it. It's the signal to back up and find the moment that makes the lesson obvious.
- **Adverb-dependent emotion.** *"He was deeply moved," "she was truly hurt."* The voice doesn't soften or intensify with adverbs — it shows the thing directly. When adverbs are carrying emotional weight, the writing has drifted.
- **Cold open without orientation.** A chapter that drops the reader into a scene without any anchor is a drift warning. One sentence of setup is not a spoiler — it's a contract with the reader.
- **Stoic terms, or any elevated vocabulary, without plain-English translation on first use.** *phantasia*, *prohairesis*, *hegemonikon*, etc. — any of these appearing without an immediate plain explanation is a failure to serve the reader. The same applies to ordinary-but-elevated English words a 6th grader hasn't met (*posterity*, *equanimity* used without its definition). The test is the word's difficulty, not its origin: would a 12-year-old need a dictionary?
- **"Undefended," "infected/contagion" used as an unqualified threat, "absorb" as invasion, or "defense" applied to her.** This vocabulary family reliably shows up when a hook or subject line is being sharpened for punch — the dichotomy-of-control point ("her mood isn't yours to govern") gets rewritten as danger language. Check any chapter or post drafted from the dichotomy-of-control framework for this drift specifically, especially in Substack subject lines and opening hooks, where the pull toward combative language is strongest. The framework is currently tagged to six chapters — by slug, per CLAUDE.md Rule 6: `the-three-second-window`, `stop-outsourcing-your-peace`, `the-discipline-of-not-reacting`, `the-end-of-scorekeeping`, `when-your-marriage-feels-unfair`, `when-faith-values-or-ambition-diverge` (Ch1, 2, 3, 7, 8, and 20 as the outline currently numbers them; see `okf/frameworks/dichotomy-of-control-in-marriage.md` for the live list).

## Reading Level and Accessibility

Target: 6th-grade prose with ideas intact. Sixth-grade vocabulary, short active sentences, no undefined jargon. The ideas stay complex — the language doesn't. Stoic terms are always translated in plain English at first use, inline, same sentence or the next (e.g., "*prohairesis* — the part of you that chooses before you react"). If a sentence runs more than 20 words, split it unless the length is doing deliberate rhythmic work. The same goes for sentences that stack several items before telling the reader what they add up to: if the payoff only arrives after a list, ask whether the reader needs the label at all. The concrete details often carry the point on their own. Common words always beat precise words when both convey the same meaning. The test: could a motivated 12-year-old follow this idea without stopping? If not, simplify the expression — not the thought.

**Word count bias:** When a chapter can go deeper on its core mechanism without padding, err longer rather than shorter. The upper bound of the target range is a guide, not a ceiling. A chapter that ends before the idea has fully landed is the failure mode to avoid — not one that runs a few hundred words past the target because there was genuinely more to say.
