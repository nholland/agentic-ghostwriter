## A. The loop with no finish line

**A stranger sees this and takes away:** learning your wife is a loop you keep
going round, ask, remember, ask again, and at its centre is who she is now, not a
finish line you crossed at the wedding.

**Carrier:** a closed circle of three arrows with no start and no end. Three
stations on it (ask about her, remember the answer, ask about it again), and "who
she is now" at the centre, the thing the loop keeps circling. The absence of an
endpoint is the argument: the chapter says he "treated the wedding as proof you'd
finished."

**Chapter's own images used:** the garden. "The following Tuesday, you ask where
the first bed is going ... It happened because you finally heard about the garden,
and then asked about it again." And "You were her suitor before she said yes, and
that job stayed open."

**The misreading a skeptic would make:** "a routine for questioning my wife": the
loop reads as a system or a chore on a schedule, not curiosity. It also says
nothing about the dating contrast, so the "you already stopped" half of the
Conversation sentence rests on the takeaway alone.

## B. The questions stopped; the unknown did not

**A stranger sees this and takes away:** when you were dating you knew little
about her and kept asking; now you know more, she has kept changing, and the
questions have stopped. What ended was your curiosity, not the mystery.

**Carrier:** the same figure twice, left and right. She is the outer circle; what
you know of her is the grey core. Dating: a small core inside a large open circle,
with four arrows pointing in at it (the questions). Now: a larger core, labelled
"at twenty-six," inside a circle that has grown, and **no arrows**. The drawn
relationship is the arrows present on one side and gone on the other while the
open space remains. This is the author's own note in the brief: *"how did you
already have the answers when you were dating? ... the mystery of not knowing."*

**Chapter's own images used:** "You asked her questions because you wanted the
answers." "You know what she liked at twenty-six." "She's changed across the years
you spent in the same house." "What stopped was your curiosity about who she is
now."

more demanding, rather than simply changing. The grey core could also read as
"what you lost." The unlabelled arrows carry "questions" only through the caption
"you wanted the answers"; a cold reader should confirm they read as asking.

## C. Two routes to Saturday

**A stranger sees this and takes away:** you can't plan something she'd love this
Saturday out of what she liked when you met; you have to ask her who she is now.

**Carrier:** one target (the chapter's own test, "one thing she'd be excited about
this Saturday") and two arrows aimed at it. From memory, "what she liked at
twenty-six," a dashed arrow that stops short against a bar. From "So you ask her."
a solid arrow that arrives. The drawn relationship is reach: memory falls short,
asking gets there.

**Chapter's own images used:** the date you can't plan. "The first place that
comes to mind is the Italian restaurant she loved when you were dating ... You know
what she liked at twenty-six. You can't name one thing she'd be excited about this
Saturday. So you ask her."
