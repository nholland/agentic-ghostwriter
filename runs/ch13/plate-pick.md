# Ch13 plate pick (gw-plate Stage 2)

Reader Panel, 2026-09-30 (date from the session environment; no shell here to read the clock to the minute).
Order kept: I looked at the three thumbnails first and wrote the ten-second reads below before opening `distillation.md`. I did not read the chapter prose or `plate-concepts.md`.

## Ten-second reads (the plate alone, a married man on his phone)

**A. Cycle: Ask about her. -> remember the answer -> ask about it again, around "who she is now". Caption: "You were her suitor before she said yes, and that job stayed open."**
Takeaway: "Keep asking her about herself and remember what she says, the way you did when you were courting."
- Skeptic misread: it looks like a routine or homework, a quiz loop that treats her as a file to keep current. Nothing in the drawing says why he stopped, so a defensive man reads "more chores" and not "you went quiet".
- Layout: the labels don't match. "Ask about her." has a capital and a period, the other two are lowercase with no period. The gap in the ring beside the lower-left dot is wider than the other two, so the loop looks broken there. Nothing is crowded or off-axis.

**B. Two panels. "when you were dating": a small grey dot inside a ring with four arrows pointing in, labelled "you wanted the answers". "now": a big ring around a grey disc marked "at twenty-six", labelled "She's changed." Caption: "What stopped was your curiosity about who she is now."**
Takeaway, honestly: "Back then I was focused on her. Now... she's changed?" The drawing doesn't explain itself in ten seconds. The reader has to work out that the grey disc is his outdated picture of her, and that the missing arrows on the right are the point. Only the caption says it.
- Skeptic misread (serious): "She's changed." in large italic under the "now" panel reads as the husband's complaint, "she isn't the woman I married". That is the defensive reading, and the plate prints it in the biggest type in the panel. The caption corrects it, but he reads the panel first.
- Layout: the lowercase "you wanted the answers" next to the capitalised "She's changed." is inconsistent. The left grey dot has no label, so it's unclear what it is. Both rings are unlabelled, so he can't tell whether a ring is her or him. It reads as two diagrams, not one before-and-after.

**C. A box: "one thing she'd be excited about this Saturday". A solid arrow reaches it from "So you ask her." A grey dashed line from "what she liked at twenty-six" stops short with a stop-bar. Caption: "She's changed across the years you spent in the same house."**
Takeaway: "What I know about her is old and doesn't get me to what she wants now. Asking does."
- Skeptic misread (mild): "she changed and didn't tell me". The caption points back at him ("in the same house"), so it is gentler than B. A second risk is that "So you ask her." reads as logistics (ask her what she wants to do Saturday) and not as curiosity.
- Layout: the dashed line ends in open space halfway to the box. The tilted stop-bar reads a bit like a hammer or a broken stroke. The solid arrow lands right of centre on the box. "So you ask her." opens with "So" and has no premise in front of it. The left label is two lines and the right is one, so the baselines don't match. Everything is legible, nothing is off-axis, and nothing is crowded.

## Against the distillation

Conversation sentence: *"You can love your wife and keep doing things for her, and still have stopped being curious about who she is now."*

**Pick: C.** It is the only drawing that shows, with no caption, a man still acting for her but aiming at who she used to be, and it lands on the chapter's own Challenge (this Saturday). That is the sentence in his words.

**Runner-up: B.** Its caption is almost the Conversation sentence word for word ("What stopped was your curiosity about who she is now"). But the drawing doesn't carry it in ten seconds, and "She's changed." invites the defensive misreading.

A is the clearest drawing and the least accusatory, but it draws Practice 1, the remedy. It doesn't show the diagnosis the sentence names.

## Edits to carry into the C draft (Stage 3)

1. Label the dashed line with an action, not just a fact. For example, add "the plan you made" above "what she liked at twenty-six". The dashed line then shows the first half of the sentence (you keep doing things for her) aimed at the old her, which is the whole point.
2. Relabel "So you ask her." to "ask her" (or "ask her, then ask again"). This drops the dangling "So" and ties it to the Challenge's second ask.
3. Make the dashed line run most of the way to the box and stop visibly short. Replace the tilted stop-bar with a clean gap or a small perpendicular tick, so it reads as "falls short", not as a hammer.
4. Aim the solid arrow at the centre of the box's bottom edge.
5. Consider changing the caption to the second half of the sentence ("You still do things for her. You stopped asking who she is now."). The current caption is accurate but restates the drawing and leaves "she's changed" as the subject.
