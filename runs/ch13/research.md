## The chapter the author commissioned

Author's latest direction governs the use of all earlier material:

> As for chapter, we are in the Sun Arc, so the goal is to give warmth and avoid the traps of lack of warmth. This is a guide to men for a great marriage.

He then asked to move to draft. The chapter should make a capable husband want to turn toward the woman he loves. Give him ordinary ways to express interest and enjoy her company. The absence of warmth supplies recognition and stakes, not the chapter's dominant emotional register. Avoid a prosecution of inattentive men, a diagnosis of damaged marriages, or an account of studies.

The outline's three obligations remain: commitment can wrongly feel like the end of pursuit; losing attention can make a spouse feel unseen; ongoing pursuit means specific curiosity about who she is becoming. Sun × Wisdom names the larger framework: knowing what love requires now, then deliberately offering it. The pre-commitment paradox can be explained briefly, without claiming every man follows it. Pursuit continues as an invitation to connection, not pressure for a response.

## Author material to carry into prose

These are exact interview words, supplied for meaning and voice. They describe recognizable patterns; none is documented first-person history.

> Your wife is always evolving, as are you. The key is to evolve together.

> My personal frame is the number of times you say "Love you" with no true thoughts behind it. The number of times you ask how her day was and are content with "fine". The times you and her start watching different shows, listening to different podcasts, and neither has interest on the others choices.

> When a child brings you a hand drawn picture, you express joy and wonderment, but can barely look away from your phone when your wife shares about her day. You don't give her your full attention. You don't remember things. You aren't interested, and she mirrors to be less interested in you.

He also brought Heraclitus's changing-river idea, initially calling it Stoic, and Esther Perel's discussion of familiarity and boredom. Correct attribution below preserves his intended insight.

Use the child's drawing contrast as evidence of a capacity the reader already has: he knows how to look up, receive something warmly, and be pleased that someone chose to share it with him. Give that generous attention an adult expression with his wife. Do not scold him, ridicule his parenting, suggest performing delight, or infantilize his spouse. Follow recognition promptly with the pleasure and possibility of giving attention.

The phrase “love you” need not become suspect merely because it is familiar. The author's concern is saying it while rarely bringing interested attention to the person. Repetition and warmth can coexist. Likewise, “fine” may really mean fine, or may mean tired or not ready to talk. Invite a fuller answer without interrogating or demanding access.

## A draftable progression

This is a suggested progression, not additional book scope or required headers.

1. Open inside the reader's familiar daily exchange, with 1–3 orienting sentences before any scene. Affection is present; the opportunity is to be more interested in the person hearing it. Contrast automatic exchanges with how naturally he receives the child's picture. Keep the opening warm and recognizable.
2. Name how commitment can make learning feel finished. Briefly show the outline's date premise: after years together, you consider inviting her somewhere and realize your answer about what she would enjoy belongs to an earlier version of her. Use direct-address illustration or a modest conditional, not a named invented couple or “this happened to me.” The precise twelve years is optional illustration, never biographical evidence.
3. Turn through Seneca's treatment of Heraclitus: people continue changing too. His wife is not the only changing person. The author's marriage application is the opportunity to keep discovering each other, not a frightening claim that she has become a stranger.
4. Make continued curiosity pleasurable and practical. Different podcasts, shows, or interests give him something to ask about; he need not adopt her tastes to enjoy learning why she likes them. He can share what has caught his own imagination as well. One shared unfamiliar experience may extend this, but avoid another prescription to schedule dates.
5. Show warmth in the actual exchange: put the phone down, listen to the answer, respond to the part that matters to her, welcome good news, and return to something she shared. Select two or three concrete actions and let them unfold naturally rather than presenting an exhaustive checklist. A short example question may be newly composed as illustrative dialogue, never attributed to the author or his wife. Leave space for tired days and later conversations; “give warmth” is not “perform constant excitement.”
6. Close with one sentence in the author's own voice and a concrete moment of renewed attention. No borrowed quotation as the final line, second ending, takeaway box, or guarantee that she will reciprocate. The next chapter can address satisfaction and comparison; no need to pre-teach it here.
