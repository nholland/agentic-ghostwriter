## Most useful new directions

### 1. Disclosure needs a receptive response

Laurenceau, Feldman Barrett & Rovine (2005), *The Interpersonal Process Model of Intimacy in Marriage: A Daily-Diary and Multilevel Modeling Approach*, Journal of Family Psychology 19(2), 314–323. Canonical: https://doi.org/10.1037/0893-3200.19.2.314 . Access: full paper text read, including abstract, methods and limitations, at https://www.affective-science.org/pubs/2005/Laurenceauetal2005.pdf .

Ninety-six married couples completed diaries over 42 days. Self-disclosure and perceived partner disclosure each predicted same-day intimacy; perceived responsiveness partially mediated their associations. This makes the response to a disclosure relevant, beyond merely asking questions. Limit: observational daily summaries cannot establish causal direction or the precise sequence inside conversations. The authors expressly allow intimacy to encourage disclosure and responsiveness, too. The predominantly European American, educated, largely rural volunteer sample limits generalization.

Candidate application: the important part of “How was your day?” is what happens after the answer. Curiosity includes showing that the answer registered. Asking increasingly personal questions while staying unreceptive is not the implied remedy. This supplements the existing Reis responsiveness citation rather than replacing it. It supports a possible reciprocal process, not a law that every wife mirrors neglect.

### 2. A distracted husband may be overloaded as well as complacent

Neff, Nguyen & Williamson (2021; online 2020), *Too Stressed to Help? The Effects of Stress on Noticing Partner Needs and Enacting Support*, Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin 47(11), 1565–1579. Canonical and full-text access: https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167220974490 ; read publisher article at https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0146167220974490 , including limitations.

In diaries from 121 married couples, greater chronic stress among husbands was associated with poorer accuracy in identifying their wives' support needs. When husbands did perceive a need, their own daily stress was associated with reduced support provision. The authors caution that direct gender-difference tests were not significant: do not turn separate significant/nonsignificant findings into a universal men-versus-women claim. Relatively happy early-marriage sample, single-item diary measures, and nonexperimental design constrain inference.

Candidate application: distinguish failing to notice from noticing and failing to respond. Making room for attention may require addressing competing demands. This avoids diagnosing every flat exchange as lack of love. No matching Neff/Nguyen study found in the OKF search. Coordinate with Ch11's burden/endurance terrain; keep Ch13 focused on attention.

### 3. Stress may change what you notice, not just how much

Neff & Buck (2023; online September 26, 2022), *When Rose-Colored Glasses Turn Cloudy: Stressful Life Circumstances and Perceptions of Partner Behavior in Newlywed Marriage*. Canonical: https://doi.org/10.1177/19485506221125411 . Access: publisher abstract read at https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/19485506221125411 ; full article restricted, not read.

A diary study of 79 newlywed couples linked greater recent stressful life events to greater sensitivity to fluctuations in negative partner behavior, without parallel sensitivity to positive behavior. Those participants also perceived more negativity overall. This does not show that their observations were imaginary, or establish that stress caused perceptual differences.

Candidate application: “I notice her constantly” can coexist with little curiosity if attention is concentrated on mistakes. Keep as secondary support; the support-needs study above is more directly useful. No exact source reuse found.

### 4. Shared novelty has experimental support—but already belongs to Ch12's research

Aron, Norman, Aron, McKenna & Heyman (2000), *Couples' Shared Participation in Novel and Arousing Activities and Experienced Relationship Quality*, JPSP 78(2), 273–284. Canonical: https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.78.2.273 . Access: full PDF text accessed at https://files.blogs.baruch.cuny.edu/wp-content/blogs.dir/7309/files/2019/11/Aron_Norman_Aron_McKenna_Heyman_2000.pdf .

Surveys and three laboratory experiments examined shared novel/arousing activities. In the experiments, a seven-minute novel/arousing joint task produced larger immediate improvements in experienced relationship quality than a mundane task. The result supports a short-term causal effect of the assigned activity; it does not demonstrate lasting marital repair or establish that an expensive date is required. Novelty and arousal were combined, so do not isolate novelty as the sole active ingredient.

Candidate application: evolving together may include doing something neither partner already knows how to do. However, the source is already in `okf/citations/aron-et-al-2000-novel-arousing-activities.md` and `chapters/ch12/research.md`. Update that existing citation's stale “primary NOT read” access note in the owning session if desired; do not create a duplicate. No named study match in refined chapter prose was found by the reuse scan.

## A promising finding that needs a stronger access pass

*Older but not wiser—Predicting a partner's preferences gets worse with age* (2011), Journal of Consumer Psychology 21(2), 184–191. Canonical https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcps.2010.08.001 . Publisher search-indexed abstract read; direct publisher fetch returned 403, institutional PDF fetch did not return readable paper. Evidence remains search-synthesis, not full-paper or directly fetched abstract.

The indexed abstract reports 58 younger and 20 older couples, lower preference prediction accuracy and greater overconfidence with longer relationships. Strong conceptual fit for a husband assuming he already knows his wife. Crucial limitation: age, cohort and relationship duration overlap in this comparison; it cannot establish that staying married makes an individual less accurate, or that partners changing was the cause. Do not headline “married people stop knowing each other.” This is a lead for a later verified acquisition, not necessary support for the chapter.
