# Chapter 16: Warmth Is Strength

You were handed a picture of a strong man long before you were old enough to question it. He protects. He provides. He carries things without complaining, and he doesn't talk about how he feels. If you grew up around men like that, some of it is still in you, and some of it deserves to be. That picture gave a lot of men real strength. It kept families fed and safe.

But the calm you build to get away from the worst of it can leave you far from her. Just as far as the men who yelled.

---

**The old blueprint.**

The men I grew up around fit the picture. Men were fearless. They were protectors. They were providers and they provided for their families. You can respect that, and I do.

Feelings were another matter. My sense is that most of the men I grew up around would have thought it way too weak to talk about your feelings. So a man had about three settings. You basically made demands, you had benign neutral conversation, or you kept quiet. There wasn't a lot of nuance there.

And when quiet stopped working, there was one more. A man bore things quietly or if he finally was pushed to the edge it was okay for him to lose himself in anger and blow up. That was the rule. The blow-up it called okay is the nail from Chapter 4.

In my family, that meant my grandfather and my father, the men you met in Chapter 1. Their word was the law. When they were challenged, they answered with curse words, yelling, screaming, and occasionally throwing things around the house. It was a way to show that they were powerful.

You may not have grown up with the throwing. But you probably know the three settings. Think about the last time something was really bothering you. Did you make a demand, keep the talk light, or say nothing at all?

---

**The thin line.**

That old picture is fading now, and a lot of men don't know what replaced it. It's very challenging to know, in 2026, what being a real man means when it comes to communicating. You've heard that you should open up. You've also absorbed, from somewhere, that opening up costs you.

It's a thin line between being vulnerable and being detached.

Many men live right on that line. Share too much of what you feel, and you fear you'll look weak and whiny. Hold it all in, or let it come out as temper, and you fear you'll come across as dangerous. A man nobody can get close to. So you stand still and say as little as possible. That feels safest. It also lands you right back in the old blueprint, keeping quiet.

The way I see it, you have a lot of lost boys who don't know how to navigate: What does it mean to be a man?

Some of those men find a philosophy that seems to answer the question. I did.

---

**What Stoicism gave me.**

When I found Stoicism, the first thing it gave me was a name for what I'd watched growing up. It put a label on the fact that being angry was a state of temporary madness. You were effectively out of control, and a man who is powerful and strong is in control. A man who is effectively slavish to his emotions, doing whatever they tell him, is weak.

Seneca was a Roman statesman and adviser to an emperor, and he wrote a whole book on anger. He opens it by passing along what thinkers before him had said: "Certain wise men, therefore, have claimed that anger is temporary madness." (*De Ira* I.1.2, Basore trans.)

The men I grew up around thought the blow-up proved how strong they were. Here was a book nearly two thousand years old saying the blow-up was a man losing his grip on himself. Stoicism also taught me I didn't have to give in to the emotions of a situation. When someone around me was upset, I could stay calm.

If you came to Stoicism the way a lot of men do, that's probably what hooked you too. Calm feels like strength, and most of the time it is. But you can carry it too far. I did.

---

**Said out loud.**

When I first got into Stoicism, one of the first arguments I had with my wife led to her being angry at me and me sitting there quiet. I don't remember what it was about. After she was done engaging, I simply looked at her and said, "I can't control you. I can only control me and I don't see things the way you do."

Chapter 1 told the quiet version of this mistake, the one that ran in my head. This was the version I said to her face. I was using a lot of Stoic phrases in the actual argument, lines about what I could and couldn't control. That line was one of them.

Then she answered.

"If I have a man who doesn't care how I feel, if I have a man who believes that he's in a relationship by himself, then he will find himself in a relationship by himself."

She was very quick to communicate. She did not want to be married to a stone.

Her words landed. When she started getting upset about the things I was saying, I had a moment of reflection. I realized I had to be doing something wrong. At the end of the day, philosophy is about living a set of truths that should yield a good life. And whatever we were arguing about, it wasn't a matter of principle. My behavior and the way I was talking to her had made the situation worse. I needed to update how I was thinking about it.

She had heard my Stoic phrases and concluded that I didn't care about her. When those phrases are in the hands of someone who is unskilled or uneducated about Stoicism, that is a reasonable conclusion. I was the unskilled one.

I thought I was getting stronger. I was just getting further away.

Being self-controlled in a situation is different than being detached or even smug. Sometimes people wear their philosophy and their religion like a moral superiority cloak.

If you've ever said "I can't control you" to your wife, or anything close to it, you know how calm it feels from your side of the room. Think about how it sounds from hers.

---

**Compassion keeps you reachable.**

So what does your calm look like when it serves her instead of shutting her out?

For me, the answer is compassion. I think empathy is the wrong emotion here, because it gives your feelings over to somebody else. You end up feeling whatever she feels. You can feel compassion for somebody without taking on their emotions. And compassion is a much stronger word because you don't want the other person to feel upset, anxious, or sad. You want them to be in their highest state.

Chapter 2 dealt with one way this goes wrong: the pull to match her mood. The other way is staying so steady that she can't reach you. Compassion fixes both.

People want connection, and my wife craved connection. I crave connection with her. Of course, I don't want my wife unhappy.

There's a Stoic reason under that, too. The Stoics saw every person as a fellow citizen of one city, and that city is the whole world. The name for this idea is the cosmopolis, Greek for "world-city." Marcus Aurelius, the Roman emperor, said it about himself: "my city and country, so far as I am Antoninus, is Rome, but so far as I am a man, it is the world." (*Meditations* VI.44, Long trans.) Antoninus was one of his own names.

The way I read it, we are all here for each other as part of the cosmopolis. We want to find happiness and joy and success working with each other. That's the virtue of justice, the one Chapter 1 called Kind. It means acting from real care for the people around you. The next step is my own. If you owe justice to a stranger in that city, you owe it first to the woman you married.

Your steadiness is for her.

Chapters 2 and 5 named the preferred indifferents: things outside your control that you'd still rather have go well. Her happiness is one of them. Her hard day is a dispreferred one, something you'd rather she didn't have. As I read it, dispreferred and preferred are states where you exist almost all day long. It's the absolutes of detachment or attachment that you effectively get to only a few times. Attachment means your peace rides on how her day goes. I don't own how she feels. I do prefer to make her feel valued. I do prefer to make her feel loved.

Stoicism gets a bad rap because a lot of times people can seem like they are unemotional or detached. I think that is a sign of immaturity in the study of the philosophy. You can be steady and warm at the same time. Your wife needs both.

---

**What you put in.**

I teach my sons a frame that isn't mine. Stephen Covey made it famous in *The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People* (1989): the emotional bank account.

People want to feel like their relationship is an investment not a burden. I tell my boys that this is true with their teachers, with their coaches, with their girlfriends. And I believe it's critical for husbands to understand the same thing.

Every small kindness is a deposit. You keep making them over a long time. There is inevitably a time when you will need to make a withdrawal. Sometimes the sun goes down. It could just be for a short night. It could be because it's a long winter. What gets you through a hard stretch is what you put in before it got dark.

So look back. Over the last few weeks, the last semester, the last year, what have you put in? For me, it's small things. Saying good morning to my wife when I'm traveling. Making her coffee. Rubbing her back. Picking up a little gift for her while I'm out. They add up. It's just about the credits.

You want to be in the green with everyone, putting in more than you take out. That means your kids, your parents, your siblings, the people you work with, and your wife. But you should have the most credits with your wife because she is your number one.

This frame only counts what you put in. Her side never goes into it, and neither does anything you think she owes you. Counting your side against hers is the private tally Chapter 7 warned against. The way I see it, the bank doesn't ever owe you money. Keep your look back to yourself.

My grandfather knew this before I did. He told me once, "Don't forget your wife when you have kids." I asked him what he meant. He said the kids will grow up and leave to live their own lives. "You will not be there for them but your wife will always be there so treat her like your number one priority."

The warmth you put in now is what your marriage lives on when the long night comes.

---

**From me to us.**

Being happy as an individual is extremely different than being happy in a relationship. Being happy in a relationship requires thinking about both people, and realizing it's a joint decision. The health of the whole is more important than the health of the individual.

Start with equanimity, the calm and steady inside that Chapters 1 and 2 named. When you are in equanimity as an individual, you are at peace. I want our marriage at peace.

The Greeks also had a word for excellence. For the Stoics it meant excellence of character: being wise, just, brave, and self-controlled. The word is arête. I read it as being at your highest self. You're basically maximizing all of your potential. I want our marriage at its best.

And they had a word for a life going well as a whole, the kind of happiness that lasts longer than a good mood. That word is eudaimonia, usually translated as happiness or flourishing. I want our marriage flourishing.

The Stoics used arête and eudaimonia about a single person, and I use equanimity the same way. Putting all three on a marriage is my own step. But I think they apply to your relationship as well. You want yours at peace, at its best, and flourishing. You can't get there by looking after only your own calm.

I want my wife to be loved. I want her to feel desired. I want her to feel like a priority.

I feel deep joy and deep love for my wife. I love walking the block with her. I love going to the movies with her. She's my best friend.
