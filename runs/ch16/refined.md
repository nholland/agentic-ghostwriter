# Chapter 16: The Marriage in Flow

You know the stretch I mean. Nothing between you needs managing. Nobody is bracing for the next thing, and nobody is waiting for the other one to slip. You and your wife are in the same room because you both want to be there, and it's easy.

That ease is what everything so far has been working toward. The calm and the strength make it possible, and the warmth is what makes it easy.

Part I was the River: staying calm when things change. That calm keeps a hard week from turning into a fight with your wife. Part II was the Oak: the strength to carry weight, so she can lean on you when life gets heavy. Part III is the Sun, and this chapter is where the three come together. The warmth you bring her is what the calm and the strength were for. It's the capstone, the piece that sits on top of everything you've done so far.

The calm and the strength can feel like the hard part, and they are. But if you have both and still keep your warmth from her, you've done the work and she misses the best of it. Your attention and your joy are what she actually gets to live with every day.

---

**The old picture.** A lot of us grew up with a different idea of what a man is. Men were fearless. They were protectors. They were providers, and they provided for their families. You can respect that, and I do. The trouble was that it left a man nowhere to put what he felt. A lot of men I've known said nothing about what they felt and only spoke up to make a demand. A man bore things quietly. If he finally was pushed to the edge, it was okay for him to lose himself in anger and blow up.

Calm was my first fix for that, and calm can go cold. That was Chapter 1, where my calm went cold and I said nothing. Another time, I put that cold calm into words and said them to my wife.

I'd just gotten into Stoicism. Not long after, we had an argument. She was angry at me, and I sat there quiet. When she was done, I looked at her and said, "I can't control you. I can only control me and I don't see things the way you do."

She answered right away.

"If I have a man who doesn't care how I feel, if I have a man who believes that he's in a relationship by himself, then he will find himself in a relationship by himself."

She didn't want to be married to a stone.

I shouldn't have aimed that line at her at all. Saying "I can't control you" carried a smug superiority, as if staying calm made me better than her. The idea underneath it was right, and I had used it wrong. "I can only control me" is true. It's a standard I hold my own conduct to. In that argument, it had become a way to opt out of what she was feeling. It should be a mindset, not something that you repeat out loud to her.

What fixed it was turning toward her.

---

**Toward her.** Compassion is where warmth starts. Chapter 2 drew the line between feeling her mood and becoming it. You can feel compassion for somebody without taking on their emotions. Then you go to her. You sit down next to her and ask what's going on.

I think compassion is a much stronger word than it sounds. It means you want her out of the upset and the worry. You want her in her highest state, at her best, and you put your effort into helping her get there. My wife craves connection, and I crave connection with her. Compassion is how you reach her.

Reaching her is usually small. You put the phone down when she starts talking. You notice she's tired before she has to say so, and you take something off her plate. All it takes is caring how she's doing.

The way I read the Stoics, nothing is good in itself except virtue, which means the quality of your own character. The things outside it that you hope for are what Chapter 2 called preferred indifferents. They don't decide who you are. Her happiness is one of those things. *Indifferent* doesn't mean you don't care. I want my wife happy.

I'd like to conduct myself in a way that makes our marriage stronger, my wife happier, and helps us get towards eudaimonia. Eudaimonia is the Greek word for a life that's going well as a whole. It's often translated as flourishing.

Every act of compassion you carry out is a gift to your marriage.

---

**Both of you.** As I see it, a relationship is an investment, not a burden. She wants to know the time you give her is time you're glad to give.

I teach my boys that every kind of relationship, not just marriage, is one you build over a long period of time. I tell them it's true with their teachers, with their coaches, with their girlfriends. It's true for you, too, and most of all with your wife.

Chapter 6 brought in the Roman Stoic teacher Musonius Rufus, who described marriage as one shared life. I picture that life as a place the two of you own and live in every day. At the start of this book, I said a house can be structurally sound and still feel cold. The calm and the strength make it sound. The warmth makes it a home.

Even a warm place takes damage. Some of it is wear and tear from just living there. Some of it comes from inclement weather. Some comes from the parties you throw and the guests who don't take care of the place. That's what happens to a place people actually use.

Your marriage takes those costs too. Some years one of your jobs has to come first. You each come from a family, and both sides need time and patience. Someone's health takes a turn. The kids need more than either of you planned on. None of that is anyone's fault, and every one of them takes something out of the marriage.

That's why you keep investing in it. You don't wait until something is broken. You put in all along, including the weeks when nothing seems wrong.

Some of it is small. I say good morning to her when I'm traveling. I make the coffee. I rub her back. I pick up a gift while I'm out because I thought of her. Yours will be different from mine. You know what she'd notice. What makes it matter is that you did it on an ordinary day, without being asked.

Small acts of service are only part of it. The other part is showing up in the places where she knows you're with her. Remembering the dates that matter to her is one of them. Many men find anniversaries and special days hard to remember. So have a system. Write them down, in your phone or on a calendar, somewhere you'll see them in time. If you haven't done that, you haven't really thought about your investing strategy.

She wants to know she's a priority to you, and that you're in this with her. She doesn't want to ride along as a passenger in your life. She doesn't want a marriage where you're just two people sharing an address.

She looks after the place too, in her own ways. When she feels your attention, it's easier for her to give hers. When you feel hers, it's easier for you. Two people who keep putting in make it lighter for each other to keep going.

You still go first. You put in whether or not she has much to give that week. The marriage is yours too, and looking after it is your job as much as hers. As I said in Chapter 12, my grandfather told me: "Put your wife first."

So ask yourself about the life you share. Is it better now than it was a year ago?

---

**In flow.** When all of this is working, the whole marriage feels different. There's a word for this you may have heard at work: flow. You've seen *flow* before in this book. In the Introduction and Part I, it belonged to the River, as the way you keep going when life changes. Here it belongs to the whole marriage, the two of you working together.

The psychologist Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi described flow in his 1990 book *Flow: The Psychology of Optimal Experience*. He wrote about being absorbed in something that stretches you without overwhelming you. You stop watching yourself, and the doing becomes its own reward.

My sense of the word is simpler. To me, flow is when everything is going well: you're at peace, and you're at your best. Applying that to a whole marriage is my own step. So is pairing it with three words, two of them Greek terms the Stoics used. To me, they describe what flow looks like for the two of you.

The first word is one you already know. Staying calm and steady on the inside is what Chapter 1 called equanimity. For one person, equanimity means you're at peace. Your marriage can be at peace too, with two steady people in it. Then a hard day can be hard without becoming a fight.

The second word is new to this book. There's a best version of you, and a best version of your marriage. Each is the version you'd be proud of. The Greek word for excellence is arête. For the Stoics it meant excellence of character, the same virtue I mentioned earlier. I read it as being at your best. Arête, for a marriage, is the marriage at its best.

The third is eudaimonia, the flourishing from earlier: a life going well as a whole, over years, with both of you in it.

Each word comes from one part of the work. The calm you learned in Part I is what lets a marriage stay at peace. The strength from Part II, keeping your word and carrying weight, is part of what puts it at its best. The warmth in this part is what makes it a life she's glad to share with you. When you're calm and strong and loving at once, the calm, the strength and the warmth stop being separate jobs.

All three words are about the marriage. Being happy as an individual is very different from being happy in a relationship. Being happy in a relationship means you're always thinking about both people. I believe the health of the whole is more important than the health of the individual. Your peace counts, and so does hers.

A marriage is one home the two of you build and keep up together. Keep it at peace, at its best, and flourishing, and it moves into flow.

---

When your marriage is in flow, the place you share is warm and in order. Most of what that looks like is ordinary. I feel deep joy and deep love for my wife. There's nothing I want to do more than to just hang out with her. I love walking the block with her. I love going to the movies with her. On a regular weekday, that's the whole plan. She's my best friend.

I want my wife to be loved. I want her to feel desired. I want her to feel like a priority. That's what the warmth is for. Your wife should feel all of that from you, on the days nothing special is happening.

You have a lot of people in your life who need you to show up for them. Your kids. Your parents. Your siblings. The people you work with. Your wife. Every one of those relationships matters, and you should be generous with all of them.

Put the most into your marriage, because she is your number one.
