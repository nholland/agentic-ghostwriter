## B. The chapter, section by section (target ~2,000 words)

Part III's last chapter. Sun × Justice (`05-framework.md`): warmth as what is owed; failure mode
Armor, "the book's central mistake". Run-in bold section headers are allowed (≤8 words).

### B0. Opening and orientation (~120 words)

- **Move:** open inside the reader's inherited picture of a strong man (protector, provider, and the
  three settings), then 1-3 sentences of orientation: the calm he built to get away from that picture
  can leave him just as far from her. Not a lesson, not "after a fight" (01-voice: the scene comes
  later and is the author's).
- **Earn the diagnosis first** (01-voice, "honest acknowledgment before diagnosis"): the blueprint made
  sense; it gave men strength, protection, security, providing. A doctor, not a judge.
- Do not open with a quote, a definition, or the word "Stoicism".

### B1. The old blueprint (~300 words) — key point 1

Author's lines to carry (verbatim; pick, do not stack all):
- "Men were fearless. They were protectors. They were providers and they provided for their families."
- "My sense is that most of the men I grew up around would have thought it way too weak to talk about your feelings."
- "A man bore things quietly or if he finally was pushed to the edge it was okay for him to lose himself in anger and blow up."
- "you basically made demands, you had benign neutral conversation, or you kept quiet."
- "Their word was the law" (grandfather and father).
- Optional: "It was a way to show that they were powerful."

Callbacks, a clause each, never retold: Ch1's lineage (grandfather devoted fifty-plus years and quick to
yell; the father who escalated); Ch4's nails ("an apology pulls the nail"; do not retell the fence).
New here is what they did with **feelings**: demand, neutral talk, silence, blow-up. Do not add any
detail of yelling, breaking or throwing beyond what he gave ("curse words, yelling, screaming, and
occasionally throwing things around the house"). No nature/lioness claim (gap, section F).

### B2. Men's fear now (~200 words) — key point 2

- "It's very challenging to know, in 2026, what being a real man means when it comes to communicating."
- "It's a thin line between being vulnerable and being detached."
- "It's a thin line between having the confidence to be able to discuss what you want and the sense of being seen as weak."
- The double bind as **his fear and many men's** (Round 2): too much feeling and he fears looking weak
  and whiny; too aloof or volatile and he is seen as dangerous and unapproachable. Never "women see men
  as ...". "Lost boys" is his phrase; usable once, as his, with no number and no study.
- Two "thin line" sentences are the same shape; use one (device cap).
- Scope words: "a lot of men", "many men" (01-voice bans universal claims).

### B3. What Stoicism offered, and how he misused it (~500 words) — key point 3, the central story

**(a) What it gave him.** His realization first, then the old text:
- "it put a label on the fact that being angry was a state of temporary madness. You were effectively out of control and a man who is powerful and strong is in control. A man who is effectively slavish to his emotions is weak"
- Then Seneca, house edition, the one sentence: "Certain wise men, therefore, have claimed that anger is temporary madness." (*De Ira* I.1, trans. Basore). Basore's English matches his phrase word for word; that is the beat. **Seneca reports the line from "certain wise men"; do not write "Seneca called anger..."** Ground Seneca in a clause (a Roman statesman and adviser writing a book on anger). Optional second fragment, separately: "unmindful of ties" (anger forgets the people you are bound to). See `/citations/seneca-de-ira-1-1-temporary-madness.md`.
- Also his: "I don't have to give in to the emotions of a situation. When someone is upset I am able to remain calm and balanced." (paraphrase is fine).

**(b) The overcorrection, said aloud.** Told from inside, the moment before he knew better
(01-voice "stage your own mistakes in real time"):
- Setup (his words): "one of the first arguments I had with my wife led to her being angry at me and me sitting there quiet. After she was done engaging, I simply looked at her and said,"
- His line: "I can't control you. I can only control me and I don't see things the way you do."
- Her answer: "If I have a man who doesn't care how I feel, if I have a man who believes that he's in a relationship by himself, then he will find himself in a relationship by himself."
- His gloss: "She was very quick to communicate." "She did not want to be married to a stone." **"Stone" is his summary, not her words: no quotation marks on her side for it.**
- "I was using a lot of the Stoic phrases in the actual argument and it created a problem where my wife misinterpreted that I did not care about her."
- "in the hands of someone who is unskilled or uneducated about Stoicism, that is a reasonable conclusion."

**(c) The correction.**
- "Being self-controlled in a situation is different than being detached or even smug. Sometimes people wear their philosophy and their religion like a moral superiority cloak." The cloak is a second image; use the line once, do not extend it.
- **Ah-ha here**, at the turn, not as the close: "I thought I was getting stronger. I was just getting further away." (Publisher-drafted, approved via outline.)
- Optional Stoic anchor for the correction (pick at most one of this and the cosmopolis quote in B4):
  Epictetus, *Discourses* III.2 (Long): he ought not to be free from feeling "like a statue" but should
  keep up his relations "as a son, as a father, as a citizen." Long's sentence carries bracketed Greek
  glosses; quote only the two clean fragments shown, or paraphrase without quotation marks. It echoes
  "stone" closely; that is the case for it and the risk (image creep). `/citations/epictetus-discourses-3-2-not-like-a-statue.md`.

Ch1 printed the **internal** version of this period and "My wife thought I'd gone somewhere she couldn't
reach." Do not reprint that line or Ch1's "self-command / self-removal" pair; a clause of callback at
most (a pointer back to Chapter 1, not a retelling). New here: said aloud, his added clause, and her answer.

### B4. Compassion, and why he wants her well (~330 words) — key point 4

- "you can feel compassion for somebody without taking on their emotions."
- "compassion is a much stronger word because you don't want the other person to feel upset, anxious, or sad. You want them to be in their highest state."
- "People want connection and my wife craved connection. I crave connection with her"
- "of course, I don't want my wife unhappy." (Round 2 wording; the outline prints it with a capital "Of".)
- Why (his reason): "one of the Stoic principles is that we are all here for each other as part of the cosmopolis. We want to find happiness and joy and success working with each other. We are here through the virtue of justice"
  - **Gloss for cosmopolis** (plain English, first use): the Stoic idea that every person is a citizen of
    one world-city, bound to the others by shared reason; justice is what each member owes the rest.
    Optional Marcus, Long: "my city and country, so far as I am Antoninus, is Rome, but so far as I am a
    man, it is the world." (*Meditations* VI.44). "Cosmopolis" is not Marcus's word; do not put it in his mouth.
    `/citations/marcus-aurelius-meditations-4-4-and-6-44-cosmopolis.md`. Justice was named as "Kind" in Ch1's four virtues: a clause of callback fits.
- Preferred indifferents as daily life (callback; Ch2 and Ch5 already defined the term): "Dispreferred and preferred are states where you exist almost all day long. It's the absolutes of detachment or attachment that you effectively get to only a few times." And: "I do prefer to make her feel valued. I do prefer to make her feel loved".
- "Stoicism gets a bad rap because a lot of times people can seem like they are unemotional or detached and I think that is a sign of immaturity in the study of the philosophy."
- **Not Ch2 again.** Ch2 owns the definition ("You don't have to feel what the other person is feeling. You have to see it and recognize it"), the soup, emotional contagion, and its own anti-armor paragraph ("Governance as armor. That's not this."). Ch15 printed one compassion line. Here compassion is the other failure's cure: not absorbing her (Ch2) but going unreachable.
- **Do not claim the Stoics rejected empathy.** Inbox #146: the Stoics reject pity, not empathy. "Empathy is the wrong emotion" is his view ("I think"); present it as his. Optional modern clause: Singer & Klimecki 2014 distinguish empathy (sharing a feeling) from compassion (warm concern); search-only, name it, no numbers, no wording.

### B5. The bank of credits (~450 words) — key point 5, the one image

Order matters: name the frame and **credit it** before or at first use, then make it his.
- Credit (safe wording): Stephen Covey made the "emotional bank account" famous in *The 7 Habits of
  Highly Effective People* (1989). Not "Covey coined". Gottman: omit, or a clause. `/citations/emotional-bank-account-covey-gottman.md`.
- His version (verbatim options):
  - "people want to feel like their relationship is an investment not a burden."
  - "I teach my boys that relationships of all types, not just marriage, are ones where you have to invest in the relationship over a long period of time."
  - "constantly you have to put in deposits and those deposits begin to build up because there is inevitably a time when you will need to make a withdrawal."
  - The one sun mention: "sometimes the sun goes down. It could just be for a short night. It could be because it's a long winter"
  - The audit: "if you think back over the last whatever period, the last few weeks, the last semester, the last year, what credits have you put into the bank of the relationship?" His examples: good morning to his wife when traveling, making coffee, rubbing her back or her feet, a gift picked up while out.
  - "It is not about the act of service or the gift. It's just about the credits."
  - Green everywhere: kids, parents, siblings, work, wife; "you should have the most credits with your wife because she is your number one." **Do not open this with "When you are the sun"**: Ch15 printed "When you are the sun, people come to you to feel good."
- **Not a tally** (must appear): "the bank doesn't ever owe you money. You put your own money into your own bank account". Optional, his joke, use once if at all: at zero the account closes; below zero it "starts charging you interest, which could be a metaphor for divorce if we were trying to be funny." Every deposit in the prose is **his**; never her deposits, never a comparison, never what she owes (Ch7's argument: "the running tally in your head" fails). Ch7 also says you can't list the small things; the audit asks him to recall his own, for himself, never to present to her.
- The grandfather (second story): "When you have kids they will leave you." Full sense, from Round 2a, in his words: "your kids will leave you. They will grow up ... your wife will always be there so treat her like your number one priority." Do not splice with a bare ellipsis inside quotation marks across his two tellings; quote one telling or paraphrase.
- Takeaway this section must deliver: the warmth he puts in now is what the marriage lives on when the sun goes down.

### B6. Close of the Sun arc: from me to us (~250 words) — key point 6

- "Being happy as an individual is extremely different than being happy in a relationship."
- "the health of the whole is more important than the health of the individual"
- The three words, each glossed in plain English at first use (02-audience: concept before the word):
  - **Equanimity** (callback; Ch1 defined it: staying calm and steady inside): his "when you are in equanimity as an individual, you are at peace"; the relationship at peace.
  - **Arete** (first printed use in the book): Greek for excellence, being excellent at what you are for; for the Stoics, good character. His: "at your highest self". The relationship at its best.
  - **Eudaimonia** (first printed use): Greek usually rendered happiness or flourishing; a life going well as a whole, not a passing mood. The relationship flourishing.
  - Sources for the glosses: `/citations/diogenes-laertius-7-87-90-eudaimonia-and-arete.md` (paraphrase only). In Stoic texts these words describe a person; applying them to a marriage is **his** step. The prose should make clear this is what he wants for the two of them, not what the Stoics taught about marriages.
- His wish: "I want my wife to be loved. I want her to feel desired. I want her to feel like a priority."
- Joy (optional): "I feel deep joy and deep love for my wife." "I love walking the block with her. I love going to the movies with her. She's my best friend."
- **Ends positive** (Sun-arc rule).
- **Close:** exactly one sentence, the author's own, nothing after it (01-voice). Should land in the
  bank image if it can. Candidates from his words (the choice is open, section I):
  1. "you should have the most credits with your wife because she is your number one." (Round 2a, his words from "In fact I would argue that ..."; image; positive; reads directive; capitalizing "you" as a sentence start is the only change)
  2. "She's my best friend." (positive; his; not the image)
  3. "There's nothing I want to do more than to just hang out with her." (positive; his; not the image)
- No printed hand-off to Part IV (Ch15 printed none); the outline's "long winter" transition is apparatus.
