**Title (Mechanism, word for word):** Calm, strength and warmth together put the marriage in flow. Same string in the aria-label on all three.

**What the stranger must take away (Conversation sentence):** The calm and the strength were for something: when you add warmth, on ordinary days and without being asked, the two of you become one marriage at peace, at its best, and flourishing.

The sentence has three parts: (1) calm and strength are incomplete without warmth, (2) warmth is small and unasked, on ordinary days, (3) the result is one marriage at peace, at its best, flourishing. Each concept makes a different part the carrier. Excluded per the brief: sun, night, winter, garden. No money is drawn; B draws a level over time, not coins or a ledger.

## A. The capstone

**A stranger sees this and takes away:** Calm and strength alone are two posts standing apart; warmth set on top makes them one thing.

**Carrier:** completion. Left, two posts labelled THE CALM AND THE STRENGTH under an empty dashed slot. Right, the same two posts with a heavy filled stone, WARMTH, laid across them. The only change between the two drawings is the stone, so the stone is the argument.

**Chapter's own images, quoted:** "The warmth you bring her is what the calm and the strength were for. It's the capstone, the piece that sits on top of everything you've done so far." and "calm can go cold." This is the chapter's word, not its declared `metaphor_family` (bank/account); see the ruling below.

**Copy, counted (31 words excluding the title):**
- subtitle: "Warmth is what the calm and the strength were for" (distillation Lesson; prose has "The warmth you bring her is what...")
- labels: "WARMTH", "THE CALM AND THE STRENGTH" (twice, once per block)
- gloss on the stone: "without being asked" (prose)
- gloss left: "calm can go cold" (prose); gloss right: "one marriage" (Conversation sentence)
- closing: "At peace, at its best, and flourishing" (prose, In flow)
- 5 italic lines against a cap of 5.

**The skeptic's misreading:** "So warmth is the trim on top; the posts do the real holding." The stone has to read as what makes the posts one structure, not decoration. A second risk: the posts look like two people (him and her) rather than two qualities in him.

**Validation question:** Without the chapter, does the stone read as *what the posts were for*, or as an ornament?

## B. Ordinary days, a bad month

**A stranger sees this and takes away:** Small things put in on easy days, by both of you, are what the marriage lives on when a hard stretch comes.

**Carrier:** accumulation, then drawdown. One level line, heavy, rises in small even steps; above each step a small identical arrow (a deposit; identical so nobody can tell whose was whose). Then A BAD MONTH: the line falls hard but stops well above the empty baseline, because the shaded area under it, what is already there, is still there.

**Chapter's own images, quoted:** the declared `metaphor_family`: "the two of you share one joint account", "A deposit is small", "Nobody keeps track of whose was whose", "your marriage lives on what's already there between you", "Is there more between you now than there was a year ago?" The only concept that uses the anchor image, without drawing money.

**Copy, counted (34 words excluding the title):**
- subtitle: "In a marriage, the two of you share one joint account" (prose)
- labels: "ORDINARY DAYS" (prose: "on an ordinary day"), "A BAD MONTH" (prose)
- glosses: "small, and nobody keeps count" (prose: "A deposit is small", "nobody keeps count at all"); "lives on what's already there" (prose)
- closing: "Is there more between you now than there was a year ago?" (prose; the Challenge's second half)
- 4 italic lines against a cap of 4.

**The skeptic's misreading:** "Bank good behaviour so you can coast or cash it in later." The chapter says nothing put in is owed back; the plate shows no withdrawal by either person, but a stranger may still read the fall as "spending the credit." Also: calm, strength and warmth appear only in the title, so this concept draws the Lesson (small things, ordinary days) more than the Mechanism (the three together).

**Validation question:** Does the stranger see the deposits as *for the hard stretch*, and does anything on the plate tell him calm and strength are part of it?

## C. Three jobs, one marriage

**A stranger sees this and takes away:** Calm, strength and warmth are three separate things you do; together they land in one marriage that is at peace, at its best, and flourishing.

**Carrier:** convergence into one shape. Left, three separate boxes, CALM, STRENGTH, WARMTH. Right, one rounded enclosure, THE MARRIAGE, holding "at peace", "at its best", "flourishing". Each arrow crosses into the one shape and maps one-to-one; WARMTH's box and arrow are the heavy ones.

**Chapter's own images, quoted:** "Each word comes from one part of the work. The calm you learned in Part I is what lets a marriage stay at peace. The strength from Part II... is part of what puts it at its best. The warmth in this part is what makes it a life she's glad to share with you." "When you're calm and strong and loving at once, those three stop being separate jobs." "A marriage is one thing the two of you tend together." No new image: it draws the chapter's own mapping.

**Copy, counted (30 words excluding the title):**
- subtitle: "When you're calm, strong and warm at once" (distillation)
- labels: "CALM", "STRENGTH", "WARMTH", "THE MARRIAGE", "at peace", "at its best", "flourishing" (prose; plain words, so equanimity, arête and eudaimonia need no gloss because they are not printed)
- gloss on WARMTH: "without being asked" (prose)
- closing: "That's what flow looks like for a marriage" (distillation)
- 3 italic lines against a cap of 9.

**The skeptic's misreading:** "It's a checklist: tick calm, tick strength, tick warmth." A box-and-arrow diagram reads as a process chart, and the one-to-one arrows suggest three independent pipes rather than "together." It is also the least picture-like of the three; a stranger may skim it as a slide.

**Validation question:** Does the one enclosure read as *together*, or do the three arrows read as three separate deals?

Thumbnail: `concepts/C.svg` (render: `concepts/C.png`).

## For the author to rule on

- **A uses "capstone", the chapter's word but not its declared anchor image** (`metaphor_family` is the joint account). It is a second image on the plate's one visible surface. If the one-image rule is read strictly, A is out or needs your word.
- **B is the only concept on the anchor image, and it is the weakest on the Mechanism.** Calm and strength are only in the title. If B is picked, a draft could label the shaded area with the chapter's three, but that is not in the prose and would need your words.
- **None prints a Greek term.** The chapter's three Stoic words are given only by their plain-English glosses ("at peace", "at its best", "flourishing"). Say if you want equanimity, arête, eudaimonia on the plate; each then needs its gloss beside it, which costs copy.
