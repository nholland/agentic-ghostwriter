# Chapter 16: Warmth Is Strength

You know the stretch I mean. Nothing in the house needs managing. Nobody is bracing for the next thing, and nobody is waiting for the other one to slip. You and your wife are in the same room because you both want to be there, and it's easy.

That ease is what this whole book has been working toward. It doesn't arrive by accident. It arrives because of what's underneath it.

Part I was the River: staying calm when things change, so a hard week doesn't turn into a fight with your wife. Part II was the Oak: the strength to carry weight, so she can lean on you when life gets heavy and you're still standing. Part III is the Sun, and this chapter is where the three come together. The warmth you bring her is what the calm and the strength were for. It's the capstone that sits on top of everything you've done so far.

The calm and the strength can feel like the hard part, and they are. But a man who has both and holds back his warmth has done the work and kept the best of it for himself. Your attention and your joy are what she actually gets to live with every day.

---

**The old picture.** A lot of us grew up with a different idea of what a man is. "Men were fearless. They were protectors. They were providers and they provided for their families." You can respect that, and I do. The trouble was what it left a man to do with what he felt. "A man bore things quietly or if he finally was pushed to the edge it was okay for him to lose himself in anger and blow up."

Calm was my first fix for that, and calm can go cold. When I first got into Stoicism, one of the first arguments I had with my wife led to her being angry at me and me sitting there quiet. When she was done, I looked at her and said, "I can't control you. I can only control me and I don't see things the way you do."

She answered right away.

"If I have a man who doesn't care how I feel, if I have a man who believes that he's in a relationship by himself, then he will find himself in a relationship by himself."

She didn't want to be married to a stone.

I think that I shouldn't have even brought her up when I said, "I can't control you." I think that that was part of the smug superiority. The idea underneath it was right. "I can only control me" is true, and it "should be a mindset not something that you repeat out loud to another party." Said out loud to her, it sounded like a man in a relationship by himself.

What fixed it was turning toward her.

---

**Toward her.** Compassion is where warmth starts. Chapter 2 drew the line between seeing what she feels and being swept into it. The way I say it, "you can feel compassion for somebody without taking on their emotions." Then you go to her. You sit down next to her and ask what's going on.

I think compassion is a much stronger word than it sounds. It means you want her out of the upset and the worry. "You want them to be in their highest state." That's the aim of it. You want your wife at her best, and you put your effort there. My wife craved connection, and I crave connection with her. Compassion is how you reach her.

Reaching her is usually small. You put the phone down when she starts talking. You notice she's tired before she has to say so, and you take something off her plate. All it takes is caring where she ends up.

The Stoics keep the rest of it simple. They would say that "nothing matters besides virtue and everything else becomes just a preferred indifference." Virtue here means the quality of your own character. A preferred indifferent, as Chapter 2 put it, is something you hope for that doesn't decide who you are. Her happiness is one of those things. It still matters to me. Of course, I don't want my wife unhappy.

So I hold it in plain words. "I'd like to conduct myself in a way that makes our marriage stronger, my wife happier, and helps us get towards eudaimonia." Eudaimonia is the Greek word for a life that's going well as a whole. Most people translate it as flourishing.

Every act of compassion you carry out puts something into your marriage.

---

**Both of you.** As I see it, "people want to feel like their relationship is an investment not a burden." Your wife doesn't want to be the weight you're hauling around. She wants to be the thing you're glad to put yourself into.

You met Stephen Covey at the start of this book. He wrote *The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People*, and he made the phrase "emotional bank account" famous. "I teach my boys that relationships of all types, not just marriage, are ones where you have to invest in the relationship over a long period of time." I tell them it's true with their teachers, with their coaches, with their girlfriends. It's true for you, too, and most of all at home.

In a marriage, the two of you share one joint account. You both put in, and what's there belongs to the marriage. It's the same shared project Musonius Rufus described back in Chapter 6, one life run by two people.

A deposit is small. Mine look like this. Saying good morning to her when I'm traveling. Making the coffee. Rubbing her back. Picking up a gift while I'm out because I thought of her. Yours will look like your marriage. You know what she'd notice. Each one takes a minute or two. What makes it count is that you did it on an ordinary day, without being asked, because she was worth the minute.

Hers go in too, in her own ways, and they land in the same place yours do. That's what I mean by the reciprocation of attention and effort. Attention and effort come in from both sides, and both of you live on what's there. When she feels your attention, it's easier for her to give hers. When you feel hers, it's easier for you. Effort works the same way. Two people who keep putting in make it lighter for each other to keep going.

You still go first. You put in whether or not she has much to give that week, because the marriage is yours too and you don't need her permission to look after it.

Nobody keeps track of whose was whose. Chapter 7's tally kept count of your side and found her short. Here nobody keeps count at all, and nobody checks to see who's ahead. "The bank doesn't ever owe you money." Everything you put in stays there, for the marriage, and nothing you put in is waiting to be handed back to you.

So why keep it growing? Because hard stretches come. A bad month can land on you, on her, or on both of you at once. When one comes, neither of you may have much left over, and your marriage lives on what's already there between you. That's when the good mornings and the coffee turn out to matter. They're what the two of you have to go on. That's the whole reason to keep putting in on the easy days, when it feels like nothing needs it.

The question to ask yourself is about the two of you. Is there more between you now than there was a year ago?

Again, my grandfather's words ring out, the ones you heard in Chapter 12: "Put your wife first."

---

**In flow.** When all of this is working, the whole house feels different. "There's a larger concept that is popular in business that people call flow." That's the word I reach for.

The psychologist Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi described it in his 1990 book *Flow: The Psychology of Optimal Experience*. He wrote about being so absorbed in something that stretches you, without overwhelming you, that you stop watching yourself and the doing becomes its own reward.

The way I use the word is simpler, and it leans on the peace in it. "Flow is effectively when everything is going well: you're at peace and you're operating at your highest self." Applying that to a whole marriage is my own step, and so is pairing it with three Stoic words. "Again the three Stoic words are representative of flow."

You've seen the word before in this book. In Part I, it belonged to the River, as the way you keep going when life changes. Here it belongs to the whole marriage, the two of you working together.

The first word is one you already know. Equanimity, from Chapter 1, means staying calm and steady on the inside. "When you are in equanimity as an individual, you are at peace." Your marriage can be at peace too, with two steady people in it, so a hard day stays a hard day.

The second needs more room. There's a best version of you, and a best version of your marriage, the one you'd be proud of if you saw it from the outside. The Greek word for excellence is arête. For the Stoics it meant excellence of character. I read it as living up to your highest potential. Arête, for a marriage, is the marriage at its best.

The third is eudaimonia, the flourishing from earlier: a life going well as a whole, over years, with both of you in it.

You can see where each one comes from. The calm you learned in Part I is what lets a marriage stay at peace. The strength from Part II, keeping your word and carrying weight, is part of what puts it at its best. The warmth in this part is what makes it a life she's glad to share with you. When you're calm and strong and loving at once, those three stop being separate jobs.

Notice that all three words are about the marriage. "Being happy as an individual is extremely different than being happy in a relationship." Being happy in a relationship means you're always thinking about both people. I believe "the health of the whole is more important than the health of the individual." Your peace counts. Hers counts. The marriage is the thing you're both looking after.

A marriage is one thing the two of you tend together. Keep it at peace, at its best, and flourishing, and it moves into flow.

---

Most of what that looks like is ordinary. I feel deep joy and deep love for my wife. "There's nothing I want to do more than to just hang out with her." "I love walking the block with her. I love going to the movies with her." On a regular weekday, that's the whole plan. She's my best friend.

"I want my wife to be loved. I want her to feel desired. I want her to feel like a priority." That's what the warmth is for. Your wife should feel all of that from you, on the days nothing special is happening.

You have a lot of people in your life who need you to keep putting in. Your kids. Your parents. Your siblings. The people you work with. Every one of those relationships matters, and you should be generous with all of them.

You should have the most credits with your wife because she is your number one.
