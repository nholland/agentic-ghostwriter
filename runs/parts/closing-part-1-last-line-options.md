## Options

**A. River terms**
> The river goes around every stone and goes on.

9 words. Page total: **65**.
Reason: it is the opening page's own line ("It goes around it, and it goes on"),
so it adds nothing new, it closes the frame the page opened with ("The river is
you"), and "every stone" sets up three stones in the drawing.

**B. Second person**
> You go around each stone and hold your direction.

9 words. Page total: **65**.
Reason: it keeps the reader as the subject to the end, and "hold your direction"
comes from the opening page ("holds its direction anyway"), though it is the
slightly more abstract of the three.

**C. Bridge, river and man in one line**
> Like the river, you go around stones and go on.

10 words. Page total: **66**.
Reason: it names both sides of the comparison outright, so nobody has to work out
that the stone stands for what hits him, but it says "the river" a second time
after "The river is you" opened the page.

## Checks on all three

- None uses "stone drops in", "drops", "current", or "keeps its pace". Each uses
  "stone" on its own, which the opening page already has ("the stone in its path").
  If the author wants no caption word repeated at all, "stone" is the one shared word.
- Nothing new: "goes around", "goes on", "direction" and "stone" all come from the
  opening page. The grass in the drawing has no line on either page, so none of
  the options mentions it.
- No em-dashes, no domestic words, one sentence each.

## Pick

**A.** The README says this page speaks river terms "only at the start and the
close", and A is the one that does that. It opens with "The river is you" and
closes on the river, and it reads plainly on a first pass. The drawing then shows
exactly what that sentence says.
