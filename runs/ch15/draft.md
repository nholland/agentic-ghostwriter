# Chapter 15: Be Patient, Be Kind

The last chapter asked you to notice what's good in your wife and stay with it long enough to value it. That gratitude is real. It doesn't always stop you from answering her small, sharp comment with a sharper one. If you're honest, your kindness usually slips first somewhere small: one short remark, on an ordinary day. She says something with an edge about the things that need doing. You feel it land. You're tired, and before you've decided anything, your answer is already in your mouth.

---

**The zing.** My family has a word for that kind of comment. We call it a zing, and there are so many in a day it's hard to keep count. A zing is a little slight, a cutting remark. It's a critique, only small.

I didn't handle them well. Every little critique from her was met with an equal or escalated response. I had no patience, I didn't let things go, I wasn't kind.

The habit made sense, the way most habits do: you use what rewards. I'd feel tired. I'd feel frustrated that immediately she was giving me things to do. Her words felt like she was calling me lazy.

You know that pull. You're worn out, the comment stings, and answering it feels like the natural thing to do. What changed for me came slowly, over time, from living it. The question that mattered was this: who has the strength to de-escalate?

There's something else we say in our house: "Never be the worst part of somebody's day." It's a spot check, a way to make sure we're not unkind. It's also the reason not to send a zing back. A sharper answer is the quickest way to be the worst part of her day.

---

**What she's carrying.** It happens on Saturday mornings, on busy weekends. My wife wakes up with a jam-packed agenda for Saturday and Sunday to get a lot of things done. I'm tired and want to relax, or I'm just sitting down talking to the kids. She's asking for the status of things that need to be done. And sometimes she'll say something like, "We don't have time to just lay around and not do stuff." Or: "There's an infinite amount of work to get done. Just look around. I shouldn't have to tell you what to do." You've probably heard your own version of those lines.

I used to take those lines personally. Over time I realized a line like that is more a verdict on how stressed and overworked she is.

When you hear a line like that, you can ask one of two different questions. If it sounds like she's calling you lazy, ask yourself, "Is that truly the case?" If it is, fix it, and if it isn't, let it go.

Far more often, the remark says less about you than it seems. It's an outward manifestation of how she's feeling on the inside. Maybe she isn't in a good mental state. Maybe she's frustrated about something. And it reaches past your wife to the whole household. When a child has a temper tantrum because they're hungry or tired, I don't read that as a reflection of their love for you, or of how you're parenting.

One question sends you looking at yourself. The other lets you see a person who is struggling. Either way, you don't have to be offended.

---

**The long game.** I call it chess, not checkers. You stop answering every exchange and keep your eye on the long game.

Seneca, in his study of anger, put it this way: "Does a man get angry? Do you on the contrary challenge him with kindness. Animosity, if abandoned by one side, forthwith dies; it takes two to make a fight." (*De Ira* II.34, Basore trans.) "Forthwith" means at once. Seneca's angry man could be anyone, and to challenge him with kindness is to answer anger with kindness. His point is about the reply, and the reply is yours. A zing only turns into an exchange if you send one back.

So instead of giving in or retaliating, I do two things. I roll up my sleeves and start helping. Then I give her a hug, tell her I see her, and take a little time to find out if I can make her day lighter.

A 2022 study by Gordon and colleagues found that doing more of the housework, and feeling the split was unfair, went with lower satisfaction in the relationship, except among people who felt appreciated by their partner. That study was about whoever does the housework, not about a husband who helps. I read it as a reason to do both: help her, and let her know you see her.

When I let the zings go and give her kindness instead, it has an incredible impact on changing the tone and the mood of the entire house. I would rather have a good night of happiness with my wife than try to answer each exchange. You get to decide which of those you're after. I tell people, "Would you rather be right? Or would you rather be married?"

None of this is surrender. Letting a zing go doesn't mean doing whatever she says. I learned that difference through experience, and it's my own advice to myself.

---

**Usually hers.** You can be home all weekend and still not be kind. As I see it, being kind is different than just being there. Being kind is when you need to show patience. You need to show understanding. It's trying your best to make someone's day better.

When she's tired, you may be tired too. That's two people who are equally tired. Kindness is what you choose to do anyway.

For me, kindness is a foot rub when she's had a long day. It's taking one of the chores that's usually hers. It's letting her talk about her day, because it's a kindness to let her speak and get things off her chest.

Why are those kind? It isn't that they're acts of service. It's because these are normally things that she does, and I'm taking chores and work off of her plate. I'm doing something that shows I want to make her life better. You know which chores are usually hers in your house.

The reason matters. Impett, Gable and Peplau (2005), studying dating couples, found that small sacrifices made to please a partner went with better days than sacrifices made to avoid conflict. Helping to end the tension and helping to make her day better look the same from the outside. You know which one you're doing.

Seneca's work on giving and receiving is about friends and benefactors. His advice still fits a marriage: "Above all we should give willingly, quickly, and without any hesitation." (*De Beneficiis* II.1, Stewart trans.) If you're going to take something off her plate, take it gladly.

She deserves kindness every day, always, even when she's at her lowest. You don't wait for her to earn it.

---

**The best part.** The rule in our house has a second half: "You can be the best part of somebody's day."

In Book I of *Meditations*, the Roman emperor Marcus Aurelius thanks the people he learned from. Sextus is one of them. Marcus wrote that Sextus "never showed anger or any other passion, but was entirely free from passion, and also most affectionate." (*Meditations* I.9, Long trans.) The Greek behind "most affectionate" belongs to the word *philostorgia*, family love. "Free from passion" here means Sextus wasn't ruled by anger or upset, and Marcus sets it right beside "most affectionate." He was calm and warm at once.

Marcus was describing a man he learned from. That same pairing is what kindness looks like at home, in the small friction of an ordinary day.

Compassion means you see her load, you act on it, and you stay steady yourself.

So ask yourself, each day: "Was I the best part of her day?"

---

I was asking whether I'd been treated well. The better question was whether I was the best part of her day.

When the next zing lands, roll up your sleeves before you open your mouth.
