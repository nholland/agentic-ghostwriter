**Title (Mechanism, word for word):** Kindness answers the zing. Set in the Ch12 plate's letterspaced display capitals; aria-label is the Mechanism in its own case. Either case passes the `title` row.

**What the stranger must take away (Conversation sentence):** When your wife says something sharp, hear it as a sign of how she feels, help before you answer, and ask yourself whether you were the best part of her day.

The sentence has three moves: (1) hear the remark as a sign of her state, (2) help before you answer, (3) the best-part-of-her-day audit. No one drawing carries all three at equal weight; each concept below makes a different move the carrier and lets copy name the others. That is the trade the Panel is picking between.

## A. It takes two

**A stranger sees this and takes away:** When she says something sharp, don't send a sharper one back; answer with help, and the fight has nothing to bounce on.

**Carrier:** an exchange that needs two sides. Above, quiet: three jagged lines volley between HER and YOU, each bigger than the last. Below, heavy: the same small jagged line arrives, and what goes back is one smooth arc with no edge on it, labelled START HELPING. Jagged against smooth is the argument; the reply decides whether the exchange continues.

**Chapter's own images, quoted:** "Every little critique from her was met with an equal or escalated response." "A zing only turns into an exchange if you send one back." "I roll up my sleeves and start helping." No chess on this one: it draws Seneca's point in the chapter's own plain words rather than in the game image. Seneca's "it takes two to make a fight" is the idea but is **not printed**: `De Ira` II.34 is unverified, and the plate quotes no classical source.

**Copy, counted (36 words excluding the four-word title):**
- subtitle: "A zing only turns into an exchange if you send one back." (prose, The long game)
- labels: "HER", "YOU", "START HELPING" (prose: "start helping")
- quiet gloss on the volley: "an equal or escalated response" (prose, The zing)
- gloss on the second zing: "how she's feeling on the inside" (prose, What she's carrying)
- closing: "Roll up your sleeves before you open your mouth." (the chapter's last line)
- 4 italic lines against a cap of 5.

**The skeptic's misreading:** "So she gets to snipe and I just take it." The smooth arc must read as a deliberate act going back to her, not as silence or absorption. The chapter's guard ("None of this is surrender") is not on the plate.

**Validation question:** Without the chapter, does the smooth arc read as *help*, or only as *not fighting*?

## B. What she's carrying

**A stranger sees this and takes away:** Her sharp remark comes off the top of everything she is carrying, so the answer is to take something off that pile.

**Carrier:** the zing grows out of the load, and the reply lightens the load. Left: a stack of blocks, the things that need doing, with the jagged zing rising from its top, so the remark visibly belongs to her load and does not point at you. A dashed empty slot at the top of the stack; a heavy arrow carries that block across to your side. No words go back; only the block does.

**Chapter's own images, quoted:** "What she's carrying." (section label) "Over time I realized a line like that is more a verdict on how stressed and overworked she is." "you see her load, you act on it". "I'm taking chores and work off of her plate." "Off her plate." (section label). The stack is drawn as a load, not as a dinner plate: "her load" is the chapter's word. **For the author:** "off her plate" is an idiom, not a declared image, but drawing a literal plate would read as a second image family; the stack avoids that, and the closing line keeps the idiom in words only.

**Copy, counted (38 words excluding the title):**
- subtitle: "What does the remark tell you about how she feels?" (prose, What she's carrying)
- labels: "THE ZING", "WHAT SHE'S CARRYING" (section label), "YOU"
- glosses: "the things that need doing" (prose, opening and Saturday scene); "a chore that's usually hers" (prose, Off her plate)
- closing: "If you're going to take something off her plate, take it gladly." (prose, Off her plate)
- 4 italic lines against a cap of 5.

**The skeptic's misreading:** "Her zing is caused by chores, so do chores and she'll stop." The chapter says the remark is a sign of how she feels, not a bill to pay. A second misreading: the moved block reads as a burden he now carries resentfully; "take it gladly" is there for that.

**Validation question:** Does the zing read as coming *from her load* (her state) rather than *at him*, and is the moved block legible as help?

## C. Checkers or chess

**A stranger sees this and takes away:** Answering every jab back and forth goes nowhere; play the long game, whose goal is being the best part of her day.

**Carrier:** two identical boards, two kinds of play. Left, checkers: short hops back and forth along one row, quiet, going nowhere, answering every zing. Right, chess: one piece, one long heavy move up the board to a square drawn heavy, labelled THE BEST PART OF HER DAY. The long game has a destination; the hops do not.

**Chapter's own images, quoted:** "Answering every zing is checkers. Chess means keeping your eye on the long game." (the declared metaphor_family) and "You can be the best part of somebody's day." / "Was I the best part of her day?" The game image is the chapter's one anchor; this is the only concept where it carries, as the brief requires.

**Copy, counted (31 words excluding the title):**
- subtitle: "Kindness is what you choose to do anyway." (prose, Off her plate)
- labels: "THE BEST PART OF HER DAY", "CHECKERS", "CHESS"
- glosses: "answering every zing"; "the long game" (prose, The long game)
- closing: "You get to decide which of those you're after." (prose, The long game)
- 4 italic lines against a cap of 5.

**The skeptic's misreading:** "Marriage is a game and she is the opponent." The chapter uses chess for his horizon, not for her; a board invites "opponent" reading, and nothing on the plate names her as anything but the goal. Also weakest on move (1) and (2) of the Conversation sentence: it shows *don't answer every one* and *aim for the best part of her day*, but not *help first*.

**Validation question:** Does a stranger read the long move as "aim for her day" or as "outplay her"?

Thumbnail: `concepts/concept-c.svg` (render: `concepts/concept-c.png`).

## Three carriers, distinct

- A: the **reply's shape** (jagged back = escalation; smooth help = it ends). Two parties, one exchange.
- B: the **source of the remark** (her load) and **what the reply moves** (one thing off it). One person's burden, one transfer.
- C: the **horizon** (short back-and-forth hops against one long move to a goal). The chapter's game image.

No sun, floor, ceiling or pour image in any of the three. No numbers, no studies (Gordon and Impett are not on any plate), no classical quotation.

## Style matched

640 wide; height fitted (A 470, B 490, C 440); white ground rect; `currentColor` #1a1a1a with opacity for quiet tones; Georgia with serif fallback; 17px semibold letterspaced title, as in `the-thing-with-no-deadline.svg`; quiet italic subtitle and captions; letterspaced bold labels; three line weights at most per plate (quiet ~1.4, standard ~2, carrier 3.2); text-anchor set in classes only. These follow the approved Chapter plate style in `design/plates/README.md`. No style file proposed; the README section is the ratified reference.
