## 1. Negative reciprocity and escalation: who de-escalates

**What the research says, as far as search could locate it**

- **Negative-affect reciprocity.** Answering negativity with negativity is repeatedly reported as a marker of distressed couples. The usual sources:
  - Gottman, *Marital Interaction: Experimental Investigations* (Academic Press, 1979). Internet Archive holds it as a restricted lending item, `maritalinteracti0000gott`; not readable here.
  - Levenson & Gottman (1983).
  - Margolin & Wampold (1981).
  - Gottman, "Psychology and the study of marital processes," *Annual Review of Psychology* 49 (1998).

  A Gottman–Levenson chapter, quoted in a search summary, calls "more negative affect … and more reciprocity of negative affect in dissatisfied couples" the most consistent finding, replicated across labs. **SEARCH ONLY.** The exact wording and source are unconfirmed.
- **"Absorbing state."** Secondary summaries say negativity in unhappy couples behaves like a state that is easy to enter and hard to leave. That is the research version of the author's "every zing got an equal or escalated reply." **SEARCH ONLY.** The phrase is not confirmed in a primary source, so don't print it as Gottman's.
- **Who de-escalates (Gottman, Coan, Carrère & Swanson, 1998, *Journal of Marriage and the Family*):**
  - Newlyweds were followed for years.
  - The UW press release headline: husbands who accepted influence from their wives were the ones ending up in happy, stable marriages.
  - Secondary summaries add three things: husbands' de-escalation of wives' low-intensity negativity predicted stability, positive affect served to soothe the partner, and "active listening" did not predict outcomes.
  - **SEARCH ONLY.** Volume and pages are not confirmed: [PLACEHOLDER: Gottman et al. 1998 JMF volume/pages].
- **Contested, and this matters for the chapter:**
  - **Stanley, Bradbury & Markman (2000)**, "Structural flaws in the bridge from basic research on marriage to interventions for couples: Illustrations from Gottman, Coan, Carrere, and Swanson (1998)," *JMF* 62(1), 256–264. They criticize nonrandom sampling and strong causal advice drawn from correlational data. **SEARCH ONLY** (bibliographic record; argument known only from summaries).
  - **Kim, Capaldi & Crosby**, "Generalizability of Gottman and colleagues' affective process models of couples' relationship outcomes," *JMF* [PLACEHOLDER: year/volume; 2007 per my query, not confirmed by the result]. In a community sample of at-risk young couples, three things did not predict relationship status: men rejecting influence, men failing to de-escalate, and women's negative start-up. Coan & Gottman replied that the models were not meant to generalize to such samples. **SEARCH ONLY.**
- **Husbands' retaliation (Fincham, Beach & Davila, 2004)**, "Forgiveness and conflict resolution in marriage," *Journal of Family Psychology* 18, 72–81. Husbands' retaliatory motivation predicted poorer conflict resolution as reported by their wives, independent of satisfaction. This fits "who returns the slight" more closely than the Gottman claims do. **SEARCH ONLY** (abstract-level summary).
- **Flooding and the break.** Gottman Institute material describes a lab episode: conflict paused, partners rested until heart rates fell, and the conversation that followed went better. It also gives a recommended break length. I found only Institute and clinic summaries. [PLACEHOLDER: primary study for the timed-break/flooding finding, not located.] Do not print the minutes figure.
- **5:1 ratio, and its status.** The source usually cited is Gottman & Levenson (1992), "Marital processes predictive of later dissolution: behavior, physiology, and health," *JPSP* (PubMed 1403613 per search). That paper sorted couples into "regulated" and "nonregulated" by the balance of positive to negative during conflict. A search summary says the 5:1 figure itself comes from Gottman's later book-length work (1993/1994), not the 1992 abstract. Three cautions:
  - it describes behavior *during conflict discussions* in one lab's samples;
  - popular sources restate it inconsistently;
  - I found no independent replication literature either way.

  It also should not be confused with the separate "positivity ratio" literature. That literature was publicly criticized, but I did not check it this session. **SEARCH ONLY. Contested status: treat as a heuristic, not a law. Keep the number out of prose.**

**For the chapter:** the research supports the author's line, "who has the strength to de-escalate," as a description of what calmer couples do. It does not support "science says the husband must be the one."

## 2. "Fed up": cumulative irritation, sentiment override, and whether it's legitimate

- **Sentiment override.** The term is credited to Robert Weiss: Weiss (1980), "Strategic behavioral marital therapy: Toward a model for assessment and intervention," in J. P. Vincent (Ed.), *Advances in Family Intervention, Assessment and Theory*, vol. 1, 229–271. The idea: a global feeling about the partner overrides how her specific messages are heard.
  - Hawkins, Carrère & Gottman (2002), "Marital sentiment override: Does it influence couples' perceptions?" *JMF* 64(1), 193–201.
  - Secondary summaries say partners in negative override rated exchanges more negatively than outside coders did.
  - **SEARCH ONLY**, with findings from blogs. The citation was seen in a reference list on gottman.com, via a search summary.
- **Sensitization ("social allergens").** Michael Cunningham and colleagues (2005, *Personal Relationships* per coverage) report that repeated minor annoyances get stronger reactions over time. The current irritation stacks on the memory of every earlier one. This is the research twin of "fed up drives faster, more intense reactions." **SEARCH ONLY, popular coverage only.** [PLACEHOLDER: exact Cunningham et al. citation and findings.]
- **The Stoic version (PRIMARY READ, Long, Gutenberg #10661, *Discourses* II.18):**
  - The mind "being again excited by the corresponding appearance, it is inflamed to desire quicker than before: and when this takes place continually, it is henceforth hardened."
  - "when he is again lashed on the same places, the lash will produce not blisters (weals) but sores."
  - (He is writing about desire and anger together. The first quote's example is the love of money; the remedy he gives is for anger.)
- **Seneca on the same mechanism (PRIMARY READ, Stewart; Latin checked on Perseus):**
  - *De Ira* II.31: "this is why we are irritated at the smallest trifles in our own domestic affairs, and why we call our friends' carelessness deliberate injury." The Latin reads *in domesticis minima offendant*.
  - *De Ira* III.12: "A large part of mankind manufacture their own grievances either by entertaining unfounded suspicions or by exaggerating trifles."
- **Is it a legitimate signal?** Evidence that it can be:
  - McNulty & Russell (2010, *JPSP*; full title not confirmed). Per APS and ScienceDaily coverage: couples with minor problems did better when less blaming, but couples with serious, frequent problems did better when they engaged directly, even negatively. McNulty is quoted in coverage: if problems are rare, give the benefit of the doubt; if they are serious and frequent, don't look the other way. **SEARCH ONLY.**
  - Overall & McNulty (2017), "What type of communication during conflict is beneficial for intimate relationships?" *Current Opinion in Psychology* 13, 1–5, doi 10.1016/j.copsyc.2016.03.002. A review: whether a behavior helps depends on fit with the problem. McNulty is also quoted as saying indirect tactics like sarcasm don't work. That bears on zings. **SEARCH ONLY.**
  - The bundle already holds Sell, Tooby & Cosmides (2009): anger as recalibration, a bid to be treated better. Ch4 uses it.
- **Fed up is still engaged.** Karen Kayser, *When Love Dies: The Process of Marital Disaffection* (Guilford, 1993), as summarized secondarily: anger and hurt come in the middle phase, and apathy follows. **SEARCH ONLY.** No percentages in prose. This lines up with the bundle's apathy material (Wiesel; Ch9's "Avoiding Unhappiness Breeds Apathy").

**The author asked plainly whether it's ever OK to be fed up. Here is a plain answer from the evidence, not his answer:**
- Yes, as information. Being fed up can mean a real, repeated pattern needs a direct conversation, and it means you still care.
- No, as a lens. In that state every new remark arrives pre-judged and pre-sized, and the reply comes faster and hotter than the remark earned.
- His own case was "fed up with how we argued." The evidence fits taking that to her as a pattern, in a calm moment, rather than answering each zing.
- What "fed up" means to *him* is open question #5, and only he can answer it.

## 3. Why argument styles drift

- **Coercion theory.** Gerald Patterson, *Coercive Family Process* (Castalia, 1982). The theory comes from parent–child research: if one side gives in to escalating aversive behavior, the other learns to escalate. Smith Slep and colleagues extended it to couples, describing each partner as "both victim and architect of a coercive system" and building a Couple Coercion Scale; one measure record cites doi 10.1037/a0031859. **SEARCH ONLY.** Don't say Patterson studied marriages.
- **Demand–withdraw** is already in the bundle: `okf/citations/christensen-heavey-1990-demand-withdraw.md`.
- **Do habits harden?** The evidence is mixed:
  - Epictetus II.18 says yes, for anger (PRIMARY READ: "you have also increased the habit, and in a manner thrown fuel upon fire"; "If then you wish not to be of an angry temper, do not feed the habit … count the days on which you have not been angry").
  - Against the simple story: Lavner, Karney & Bradbury (2016), "Does couples' communication predict marital satisfaction, or does marital satisfaction predict communication?" *JMF* 78(3), doi 10.1111/jomf.12301. Over the first years of marriage, communication predicted later satisfaction only weakly. Most cross-lags were null, and satisfaction predicted communication at least as often. **SEARCH ONLY.**
  - The bundle's Huston et al. (2001) adds that fading affection, more than rising conflict, predicted divorce.
- "Like evolution" is the author's metaphor, not a research claim. Keep it as his.

## 5. The two phrases

- **"Playing checkers, not chess."** No known relationship idiom. The common phrase runs the other way, "chess, not checkers," meaning a situation that needs more strategy. It appears to be the author's own use.
  - What he means is open question #6, and I have not guessed it.
  - A question worth putting to him, offered only as a question: standard American checkers *requires* you to take an available capture. ## 6. Kindness as a research topic, and the benefit of the doubt

- **"Masters of Love,"** Emily Esfahani Smith, *The Atlantic*, June 2014. I couldn't reach it, so I can't quote its Gottman lines. Secondary summaries say it frames kindness and generosity against contempt and calls kindness a muscle. - **Better primary for kindness:** Dew & Wilcox (2013), "Generosity and the maintenance of marital quality," *JMF* 75, 1218–1228.
  - It defines generosity as small acts of kindness, displays of respect and affection, and willingness to forgive faults.
  - Generosity was associated with higher satisfaction and lower conflict.
  - The data are cross-sectional, so the direction of cause is unknown.
  - **SEARCH ONLY** (abstract via BYU ScholarsArchive summary).
  - Reuse note: same survey as the bundle's `wilcox-dew-2012-date-night-opportunity.md` (Ch12).

  - Distressed spouses explain it by stable traits ("distress-maintaining").
  - Longitudinal follow-ups: Fincham & Bradbury 1987, *JPSP* 53(3), 510–517; 1993, *JPSP* 64(3), 442–452 (findings not seen); Fincham, Harold & Gano-Phillips 2000, on which direction the effect runs.
