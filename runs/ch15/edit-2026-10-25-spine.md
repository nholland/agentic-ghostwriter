## Opening (unchanged)

The small remark, and your house saying: *"Never be the worst part of somebody's day."*

## 1. Hostility, met with kindness

*The zing.* What it is. Your confession: every critique met with an equal or bigger reply, and "Frankly, neither was she." Her Saturday lines. "Who has the strength to de-escalate? Somebody has to be calm..." Then **Seneca**: *"Does a man get angry? Do you on the contrary challenge him with kindness... it takes two to make a fight."* Your answer: roll up your sleeves, then a hug and "I see you."

## 2. Frustration, met with patience

*The fed-up feeling, both his and hers.* Your lines: "Feeling fed up drives faster response and more intense reactions." "I was fed up with how we argued." "That's two people who are equally tired." Patience is the gap between her remark and your reply: "Is that truly the case?" before you answer. **Seneca**, one line: *"The greatest remedy for anger is delay."* Then the long game: checkers versus chess, a good night over winning the exchange, and "Would you rather be right, or would you rather be married?" Then your thesis, put to the reader: ***Are you being the best husband you can be right now, whatever she's doing?***

## 3. Disconnection, met with warmth

*The ordinary drift. Busy days, two tired people, passing each other.* "You can be home all weekend and still not be kind." Warmth is something you do on purpose: listening to her day, a foot rub, a chore taken off her plate, the hug. **Gordon**, short: helping matters, and so does her feeling appreciated. Your Round 3 line: ***"When you are the sun, people come to you to feel good."*** **Marcus** on Sextus, calm and warm at once. Then the second half of your saying: *"You can be the best part of somebody's day."* Most men, asked whether they are, would say no. The question to ask each day: "Was I the best part of her day?"
