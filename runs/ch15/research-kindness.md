### A1. What "kindness" is in the literature, and how it differs from presence

- **No single "kindness" construct exists in couples research.** The nearest are:
  - perceived partner responsiveness: understood, validated, cared for. **In bundle:** `perceived-partner-responsiveness-reis.md` (paraphrase only, search-synthesis)
  - generosity (Dew & Wilcox, below)
  - compassionate acts (Reis et al., below)
  - the "positivity" maintenance strategy. **In bundle:** `stafford-canary-1991-relational-maintenance.md`
- **Presence alone is not the active ingredient:**
  - Reis et al. (2010): an enthusiastic listener produced more trust and perceived responsiveness than a merely *fun* interaction. **In bundle:** `reis-et-al-2010-responsive-good-news-listening.md`, page-text.
  - Girme, Overall & Faingataa (2014): shared time helped only when both were engaged. **In bundle**, Ch12.

- **Dew & Wilcox (2013), "Generosity and the maintenance of marital quality,"** *Journal of Marriage and Family* 75, 1218-1228. **SEARCH ONLY** (BYU ScholarsArchive abstract, via search).
  - Generosity is defined as small acts of kindness, displays of respect and affection, and willingness to forgive faults.
  - It was associated with higher satisfaction and lower conflict and perceived divorce likelihood.
  - The data are a national survey of couples and cross-sectional, so the direction of cause is unknown.
  - A secondary report adds that couples where only one spouse reported high generosity fared worse. That bears on "irrespective of what she does" and is unconfirmed.
  - Sample counts vary across sources (1,365 vs 1,630 couples), so keep numbers out.

### A2. Why she can feel the difference: motive

- **Impett, Gable & Peplau (2005), "Giving up and giving in: The costs and benefits of daily sacrifice in intimate relationships,"** *JPSP* 89(3), 327-344 (start page 327 seen in a lab listing; end page from my query, not confirmed). **SEARCH ONLY** (secondary summary).

### A3. "Take one of her heavy loads off her plate": dyadic coping, housework and appreciation

- **Bodenmann's dyadic coping (systemic-transactional model).** **SEARCH ONLY.**
  - The process:
    - one partner's *stress communication*, which is necessary but not sufficient;
    - the other perceives and interprets it;
    - then responds.
  - Positive forms:
    - *supportive*: helping her cope, empathy, practical help;
    - *delegated*: taking over tasks to lighten her load;
    - *common*: coping together.
  - Sources disagree on whether task-taking is "supportive" or "delegated." The distinction is timing: on request vs offered ahead.
  - Original: Bodenmann (1995), in German, *Zeitschrift für Familienforschung* (SSOAR record seen in search). [PLACEHOLDER: an English-language Bodenmann primary with the definitions, e.g. Bodenmann (2005) in Revenson, Kayser & Bodenmann (Eds.), *Couples Coping with Stress*, APA; chapter title and pages not confirmed]
  - **Bodenmann, Pihet & Kayser (2006),** *Journal of Family Psychology* 20(3), 485-493: about 90 couples over two years; dyadic coping was associated with marital quality over time. For women, both partners' coping predicted it; for men, only their own did. SEARCH ONLY. The exact title varies across listings.
  - **In bundle:** `falconier-2015-dyadic-coping-meta-analysis.md`, a meta-analytic association, figures confirmed at abstract level; `randall-bodenmann-2009-stress-erodes-relationships.md`. Both are slugged to Ch11 and neither is printed in a refined chapter (grep found no "dyadic" or "Bodenmann" in any `refined.md`).
  - **Fit with the homecoming scene:** her string of asks and remarks is, in this model's terms, stress communication. His Round 4 response (hug, "I see you", "how can I lighten your day", sleeves rolled up) is supportive plus delegated coping. That is a *description*, not a claim that the research tested his script.
- **Gordon, Cross, Ascigil, Balzarini, Luerssen & Muise (2022), "Feeling appreciated buffers against the negative effects of unequal division of household labor on relationship satisfaction,"** *Psychological Science* (article ID 09567976221081872 in its URL; volume and pages not seen). **SEARCH ONLY.**
  - Three samples collected during COVID-19.
  - Doing more housework and seeing the split as unfair went with lower satisfaction. The association disappeared among people who felt appreciated by their partner.
  - Reported as holding across gender, employment, age and relationship length.
  - Correlational. Keep the sample size out of prose.
  - **Fit:** the single best study for "relief plus recognition," which is the author's hug-and-help pairing. Stay out of Ch7/Ch8 scorekeeping: use it only for what appreciation does to a heavy load.
  - Same first author as the bundle's Gordon et al. 2012 (Ch12/Ch14), but a different paper.
- **Saxbe, Repetti & Graesch (2011), "Time spent in housework and leisure: Links with parents' physiological recovery from work,"** *Journal of Family Psychology* (April 2011; volume and pages not seen). **SEARCH ONLY** (press releases).
  - About 30 dual-earner couples with children; activities logged every ten minutes; repeated saliva cortisol over three days.
  - Wives' evening cortisol recovery was healthier when husbands did more housework.
  - Husbands' leisure went with healthier recovery, *but only when their wives spent less time in leisure*. This is the USC press release's wording (dated 2011-05-18, seen in a search summary). A Medical News Today headline glossed it as "what is good for the male is bad for the female". That is the outlet's gloss, not the study's.
  - Doing housework kept evening cortisol higher for both spouses.
  - **Fit:** almost exactly "we don't have time to just lay around." His rest and her rest were not symmetric in this sample, which supports *her* reading of the evening without making her the attacker.
  - **Caution:** a small physiological sample, and correlational. Do not say "science shows his couch time raises her stress." [PLACEHOLDER: exact form of the leisure interaction in the primary paper]
- **Pew Research Center (2007), "Modern Marriage."** **SEARCH ONLY** (Pew page not reachable).
  - The report's opening line is "I Like Hugs. I Like Kisses. But What I Really Love is Help with the Dishes" (not "housework").
  - "Sharing household chores" ranked third of nine items rated very important to a successful marriage.
  - **Keep every percentage out.** The trend comparison with 1990 came from a magazine, not Pew.

### A4. "Letting her talk about her day," being heard, understanding

- **Gordon & Chen (2016), "Do you get where I'm coming from?: Perceived understanding buffers against the negative impact of conflict on relationship satisfaction,"** *JPSP* 110(2), 239-260, doi 10.1037/pspi0000039. **SEARCH ONLY.**
  - Multiple studies, including an experiment.
  - Frequent conflict went with lower satisfaction only when people did not feel understood. Feeling understood during a conflict went with feeling better about the relationship afterward.
  - Excludes aggressive conflict and distressed couples.
  - **Fit:** his Round 4 shift (hearing a verdict on *her stress*, not on him) plus "I see you" is perceived understanding.
  - Popular blogs attach invented-looking percentages to it. Do not use them.
- **Laurenceau, Barrett & Rovine (2005).** **In bundle**, page-text, Ch13. Daily disclosure and perceived responsiveness predicted same-day intimacy in married couples. The best existing support for "let her talk about her day even when it is mundane."
- **Gable et al. (2004), capitalization.** **In bundle**, search-synthesis, Ch12/Ch13. Good news specifically, not the mundane day.

  - So do not sell "active listening" techniques as research-backed for marriage.

### B1. A "discipline of kindness": is kindness the discipline of action?

- **Epictetus, *Discourses* III.2 (Long). PRIMARY READ** (Gutenberg selection).
  - This is the text Hadot's three disciplines are built on.
  - "There are three things (topics …) in which a man ought to exercise himself who would be wise and good." The first is desire and aversion. The second "concerns the movements towards an object and the movements from an object, and generally in doing what a man ought to do". The third is assent.
  - Then: "The second topic concerns the duties of a man; for I ought not to be free from affects (… apathae) like a statue, but I ought to maintain the relations (… scheseis) natural and acquired, as a pious man, as a son, as a father, as a citizen."
  - **Fit:** the action discipline is about *relations* and duties to people. Epictetus explicitly refuses the "statue" reading of *apatheia*.
- **Hadot, *The Inner Citadel* (Harvard UP, 1998, trans. Michael Chase). SEARCH ONLY.**
  - Summaries say Hadot maps the disciplines as follows:
    - desire: universal nature, acceptance;
    - action: human nature and just conduct toward others, for the human community;
    - assent: judgment.
  - Not read. In copyright. No page or chapter locator confirmed. [PLACEHOLDER: Hadot chapter and page on the discipline of action]
- **Marcus's own triads (Long). PRIMARY READ:**
  - **IX.6:** "Thy present opinion founded on understanding, and thy present conduct directed to social good, and thy present disposition of contentment with everything which happens--that is enough."
  - **VII.54:** "Everywhere and at all times it is in thy power piously to acquiesce in thy present condition, and to behave justly to those who are about thee, and to exert thy skill upon thy present thoughts…" The phrase "those who are about thee" is the household.
  - **IX.31** and **VIII.7** state the same triad.
- **Verdict, with confidence:**
  - *Yes in Stoic terms*: kindness to his wife falls under the discipline of action (duty to relations, "social acts"). Epictetus III.2 is primary support; Hadot's mapping is secondary and unread.
  - **But the book already spends this term.** `05-framework.md`: "**Oak (Strength)** — … *Stoic anchor: the discipline of action (kathêkonta)*". The Sun's anchor is "oikeiôsis … and philostorgia, family love — Marcus Aurelius opens the Meditations praising it by name."
  - Calling kindness "the discipline of action" in Ch15 would put an Oak anchor in a Sun chapter. **Inbox item, not a desk decision.**
  - A route that keeps the architecture: "discipline" in the book's plain sense of a practice kept by repetition (*Discourses* II.18, already in Ch1 and Ch12), with the Sun anchor philostorgia supplied by Marcus I.9 (B3).

### B4. Seneca on kindness and giving (PRIMARY READ unless marked)

**De Vita Beata (On the Happy Life) XXIV, Stewart 1889.** "Wherever there is a human being, there is an opportunity for a benefit: consequently, money may be distributed even within one's own threshold".
- **Context defect:** the passage is about how a wise man with wealth gives *money*, including to slaves and freedmen in his own house. The popular line "where there is a human being there is an opportunity for kindness" lifts it out of that context, and "kindness" is not Stewart's word ("benefit").
- "Within one's own threshold" does fit the home, but only as a stretch.
- The subsection number 24.3 is search-level; Stewart prints no subsection numbers.
- Use only with the context named, or not at all.

***De Beneficiis*, Stewart 1887** (the `06-sources.md` public-domain pick). New material beyond the bundle's no-ledger concept:
- **I.6:** "What, then, is a benefit? It is the art of doing a kindness which both bestows pleasure and gains it by bestowing it … It is not, therefore, the thing which is done or given, but the spirit in which it is done or given, that must be considered".
  - Fits the author's "kindness is a certain framing of the love languages": the spirit, not the channel.
- **I.1:** "Who can be grateful for what has been disdainfully flung to him, or angrily cast at him, or been given him out of weariness, to avoid further trouble?" and "A benefit is received in the same temper in which it is given".
  - The Stoic twin of Impett's avoidance motive.
- **II.1:** "let us give in the way in which we ourselves should like to receive. Above all we should give willingly, quickly, and without any hesitation" and "The best thing of all is to anticipate a person's wishes".
- **II.2:** "however quick he may be, a man gives too late who gives what he has been asked for."
  - Answers her "I shouldn't have to tell you what to do" from the giver's side, without making her the problem.
- **II.3:** "they say 'yes' with a face which seems to say 'no.' How much better is it to join kind words to kind actions".
  - The hug *and* the sleeves.
- **II.6:** "To reproach a man at the very moment that you are doing him a service is sheer madness … Even if there be some subject upon which you wish to warn your friend, choose some other time for doing so."
  - Do the dishes without the counter-zing; save the real conversation for later (fits the prior pass's McNulty limit and Ch11).
- **Caution:** De Beneficiis is about benefactors and friends (*amici*), not spouses. Applying it to marriage is the author's application.

### B7. What the Stoics actually said: compassion, pity, being "swept up"

- **Seneca, *On Clemency* II.4-6 (Stewart 1889). PRIMARY READ.**
  - **II.4:** pity is "a disease of the mind."
  - **II.5:**
    - "all good men will show mercy and mildness, but will avoid pity, which is a vice incident to weak minds which cannot endure the sight of another's sufferings";
    - "Pity considers a man's misfortunes and does not consider to what they are due: mercy is combined with reason";
    - "no school of philosophy is more gentle and benignant, none is more full of love towards man … its maxims are, to be of service and assistance to others."
  - **II.6:**
    - "he will dry the tears of others, but will not mingle his own with them";
    - "the wise man will not pity men, but will help them and be of service to them."
  - Section numbers follow Stewart's chapter heads. Subsections unconfirmed.
- **Epictetus, *Enchiridion* 16 (Long). PRIMARY READ.** "So far as words then do not be unwilling to show him sympathy, and even if it happens so, to lament with him. But take care that you do not lament internally also."
- **Marcus I.9:** "entirely free from passion, and also most affectionate" (B3).
- **Epictetus, *Discourses* I.11:** fleeing a sick child "was not at all an affectionate act" (B5).
- **Seneca, Letter 99 (Gummere). PRIMARY READ.** A consolation to a grieving father. It opens by saying those who indulge grief "should be rebuked forthwith". It shows how harsh Stoic comfort could be. Not a model for this chapter.

**Where this leaves the author's "compassion, not empathy"**
- **Supports:** the Stoics consistently separate *helping* from *being swept into* the other's distress:
  - Seneca: help, not pity;
  - Epictetus: do not "lament internally";
  - Epictetus I.11: overwhelm that makes you leave is not love;
  - Marcus I.9: calm and most affectionate at once.
  - That is the author's line, "you have to be … strong and stable in [your] own state … to show compassion."
- **Qualifies:**
  - The Stoics did not use "empathy" (a modern word) or "compassion" as he does. The ancient target is *pity* (Latin *misericordia*). Their positive terms are mercy, mildness, benevolence, family love.
  - "Compassion" literally means suffering-with, which the Stoics would also reject. In modern research (A7) compassion includes warm feeling.
  - So "many Stoics would say empathy isn't the right muscle" is safe only as: *the Stoics warned against being carried away by another's suffering, and asked for steady, practical help instead.* Do not put "empathy" or "compassion" in a Stoic's mouth.
- **Complicates:**
  - Enchiridion 16's "so far as words" can read as performed sympathy, the armor Ch16 exists to name.
  - Seneca II.5 has a dismissive line about "old women and girls" moved by tears, and II.6's "calm mind and unaltered expression of countenance" reads cold.
  - Quote around both, and do not let the Stoic "calm face" stand for the warmth the author wants.
- **Attribution defect (standing):** Kruse (2025) presents an *apatheia* paragraph as Seneca's, and it is not in *De Ira* (`seneca-calm-not-root-out-emotions.md`). Cite it as Kruse's words only, or not at all. Use *On Clemency* II.5-6, read above, for what Seneca actually said.

## C. The coming-home moment

All research here is **SEARCH ONLY**.

- **Repetti (1989), "Effects of daily workload on subsequent behavior during marital interaction: The roles of social withdrawal and spouse support,"** *JPSP* 57(4), 651-659.
  - Air traffic controllers and their wives over three days.
  - On high-workload days the men withdrew more after work. On evenings with high spouse support, overload went with *more* withdrawal and *less* anger.
  - Repetti proposed that a supportive spouse who allows a short withdrawal helps the stressed partner recover.
  - **This complicates the scene:** a little decompression for *him* may be what kindness from *her* looks like. The research does not say the tired man should be denied the couch.
- **Story & Repetti (2006), "Daily occupational stressors and marital behavior,"** *Journal of Family Psychology* 20(4), 690-700. Stressful workdays are linked with angrier and more withdrawn marital behavior that evening. Findings are known only from later citing papers. [PLACEHOLDER: Story & Repetti findings]
- **Schulz, Cowan, Cowan & Brennan (2004), "Coming home upset: Gender, marital satisfaction, and the daily spillover of workday experience into couple interactions,"** *Journal of Family Psychology* 18(1), 250-263, doi 10.1037/0893-3200.18.1.250.
  - Per a secondary thesis: after a negative workday, husbands tended to withdraw and wives tended to show more anger.
  - Marital satisfaction moderated this, but the details were not seen.
  - **Caution:** a gendered pattern from secondhand summary. Do not use it to say "wives get angry"; that would cast her as the attacker.
- **Campos, Graesch, Repetti, Bradbury & Ochs (2009), "Opportunity for interaction? A naturalistic observation study of dual-earner families after work and school,"** *Journal of Family Psychology* 23(6), 798-807.
  - Thirty dual-earner families were filmed at home.
  - Mothers, usually home first, were greeted positively and asked about their day. Fathers, arriving later, got either a positive greeting or no acknowledgment while others were busy.
  - Couples were rarely alone together.
  - **Fit:** the reunion itself is a measurable moment, and "being ignored" is the common failure, not hostility. In the author's scene the roles differ: she is home all day and he arrives.
- **Saxbe, Repetti & Graesch (2011):** see A3, the couch and leisure finding.
- **Saxbe, Repetti & Nishina (2008),** *Health Psychology* 27(1), 15-25. In 30 dual-earner couples, wives in happier marriages showed better daily cortisol recovery after work; men did not show that pattern. Associations only.
- **"Home as haven."** The author's wife: "the world will be harsh enough."
  - Saxbe & Repetti (2010), "No place like home," *PSPB*, tied how wives described their homes to their daily mood and cortisol. Title and finding are from memory and **not confirmed** by search.
  - [PLACEHOLDER: Saxbe & Repetti 2010 citation and finding]
  - The haven idea itself is the author's family's.
- **Crossover (stress passing from one spouse to the other):**
  - **Westman (2001), "Stress and strain crossover,"** *Human Relations* 54(6), 717-751/752 (end page varies by source). Three routes:
    - direct transfer through empathy;

  - **Westman & Vinokur (1998),** *Human Relations* 51(2), 137-156. In couples, the link between spouses' depression ran mainly through shared stressors and negative interaction (social undermining). It found little support for direct empathic transfer alone.
  - **Fit, with care:** the research route by which one spouse's stress lands on the other is the *interaction*: undermining in one direction, support in the other. That is the author's choice point in the doorway.
