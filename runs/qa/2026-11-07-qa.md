## Do the later chapters read better?

**Both desks say yes, and that it is not about sentence length alone.** The Anti-Slop
Reader names seven differences. In Ch1–5:
1. fragment chains leave the reader to rebuild the cause;
2. a punchline ends nearly every paragraph;
3. "Not X. Not Y." negation stacks;
4. the wife appears as weather or as a system;
5. the triggers are generic;
6. "most men" is used as a foil;
7. mechanism nouns ("activation state", "updated her model").

The Panel adds the author's own presence. "I" appears 0.1–0.8 times per 100 words in
Ch2–11, against 2.0–3.1 in Ch12, 14 and 15. It also counts 20 "most/some/many men"
lines from the Prologue to Ch10, and none in Ch11–15.

**Ch11 is the exception:** a late chapter that reads worse. It is procedural, its
warmth review said "not yet", and none of that review's fixes have been applied.

**Keep, both desks agree:**
- the named handles: the Three-Second Window, the Four D's, the view from above, care without contagion;
- the anchor images: fence and nails, the kicked dog, the soup;
- the closes of Ch1, Ch2 and Ch4.

The Anti-Slop Reader's phrase is "Bring Ch1–5 forward, not down."

**The later chapters have their own habits:**
- instructional beat labels (Ch11, Ch13–15);
- a stock research-caveat sentence, repeated across Ch12–15;
- "Chapter N covers…" pointers;
- the "You can A and still B" shape, used four times in Ch11.

**What the early chapters need.** Both desks agree on a line-level pass on Ch1–5,
roughly 25 to 40 edits per chapter, with no structural change. The Panel adds one
first-person story each for Ch3, Ch4 and Ch5. Those have to come from an author
interview and must not be invented.
