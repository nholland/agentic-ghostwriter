# How Your Commute Ended Up Kicking the Dog
**Chapter source:** Ch2, Stop Outsourcing Your Peace ("The cascade"; Marcus quote from "The ruling faculty")   **Subject lines:** 1. You snapped at the door. The dog paid for it. 2. When the whole house is off, check who came home last 3. How five words about the dishwasher ruined dinner
**Status:** Draft, awaiting author approval. Not posted.
---

A bad day at work can make it all the way through your house and finish on the dog before dinner's on the table. You don't even have to yell. One snap at the front door does it. Nothing dramatic, just edges, and the rest of the house takes it from there without you. Most of us know what it's like to walk into somebody else's bad mood. This time the mood that wrecked the evening came in with you, and by the time it reached the dog you were upstairs changing your shirt.

You've just spent forty minutes behind a truck that brakes for every shadow, right after a meeting that should have been an email. You come through the door with your jaw still set. She asks if you called about the dishwasher. You say "I said I'd do it" in a voice that means you didn't, and also that the subject is closed. That's the whole offense, and you know it. She goes back to the kitchen, where the kids are supposedly doing homework. The next thing she says is "Feet. Off the table. Now," in a tone she didn't have at four o'clock. Your oldest gets told twice about his math, so he tells his little sister to get out of his room. His sister, now out of people smaller than her, shoves the dog off the couch with her foot. The dog did nothing. He spent the afternoon asleep in a patch of sun and has no opinion about the dishwasher.

Nobody in that house decided to hand it on. She wasn't punishing the kids, and they weren't punishing the dog. It just went from one person to the next until there was nobody left. That's how your seven-year-old ends up in timeout over a meeting she wasn't in. Marcus Aurelius was a Roman emperor who kept a private journal through years of war and plague. He wrote a lot about the part of a man that decides what a moment means. His *Meditations*, in the Haines translation, puts it this way at 6.8: "The ruling Reason it is that can arouse and deflect itself, make itself whatever it will, and invest everything that befalls with such a semblance as it wills." He's saying the part of you in charge can wind itself up or settle itself down. It also decides what a lousy commute means once you're home. The truck and the meeting weren't your call. What they turned into at your front door was.

Her mood is hers, and the kids' moods are theirs. Some nights the evening really does start with her long day, or with a kid who got left out at recess. What you bring through the door is yours. When the whole house is off around seven-thirty, the first question most of us reach for is who's being difficult. A better one is when it started and who was standing there. Going back one person at a time takes you about ten seconds. The dog got it from the youngest, the youngest got it from her brother, and he got it in the kitchen. Sometimes it ends at her day or at the school, and then you know something about her afternoon you didn't know at six. A lot of nights, if you're honest, it ends in the front hall at 6:15, with a guy still holding his car keys.

The dog, meanwhile, is back on his bed by the front door, waiting to see who walks in next.
