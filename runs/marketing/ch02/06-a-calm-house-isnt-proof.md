# A Calm House Doesn't Prove You're a Calm Guy
**Chapter source:** Ch2: Stop Outsourcing Your Peace (end of "The ruling faculty": the easy current, the peaceful house; Seneca, *Letters* 13, Gummere trans., the passage being added to the chapter this session)   **Subject lines:** 1. A quiet month with her doesn't make you a patient man 2. The small tell in how you answer her on no sleep 3. Who are you to her when nobody has slept in four days?
**Status:** Draft, awaiting author approval. Not posted.

---

Staying calm in a quiet house doesn't prove you're a calm guy. It might just mean it's been a quiet month. Nobody's sick and the kids are asleep by eight. By the third easy week you've started telling yourself you've finally got this patience thing down. Plenty of us have had that month. A few of us have said so out loud, which is roughly when the universe starts taking notes. **You can't be a boxer without being punched in the face.** A quiet month hasn't asked your patience to do anything yet, except take the credit.

Then the week shows up. The youngest gets the stomach bug at 2 a.m., and by breakfast the middle one has it too. Somebody puts a towel down in the hallway, and it stays there all week as policy. The car starts making the noise, the one that sounds expensive. Your mom calls Wednesday, very cheerful, to say the doctor just wants a few more tests. Nobody in the house has slept more than four hours, and that includes both of you. The dishwasher has been full of clean dishes since Monday because neither of you has had ninety seconds to empty it. None of it came from her, and none of it came from you. It landed on both of you at once, and it's the first week all month that has told you anything.

Seneca was a Roman statesman, and he wrote letters to his friend Lucilius about how to live. He told Lucilius that "no prizefighter can go with high spirits into the strife if he has never been beaten black and blue" (Seneca, *Letters* 13, Gummere translation). He means you don't get to trust yourself under pressure until you've been under some. The guy who gets to walk in confident, by Seneca's account, is the one who's had his teeth rattled and got up every time. A month of the kids asleep by eight was never going to teach you that. It was a great month. It just wasn't a test.

The next time something real lands, the tell is usually small. For a lot of men it's a sentence that starts with "Can everybody just." Or she asks whether anyone called the pharmacy, and you answer like she asked it to annoy you. She's running on the same four hours you are. None of that makes you a bad husband. It means you're finding out now what the quiet month couldn't tell you. The other kind of calm is plainer than you'd expect. You're wrecked, you're worried about your mom, and her question about the pharmacy still sounds like a question about the pharmacy. She's finding out the same thing about herself, by the way, in front of the same full dishwasher.

On Saturday the guy at the shop says the noise is a loose bracket on the exhaust, and it costs forty bucks.
