# A Calm House Doesn't Prove You're a Calm Guy
**Chapter source:** Ch2: Stop Outsourcing Your Peace (end of "The ruling faculty": the easy current, the peaceful house)   **Subject lines:** 1. Your quiet month at home proved less than you think 2. What to notice when a hard week lands on you both 3. Who are you to her at 2 a.m. with a sick kid?
**Status:** Draft, awaiting author approval. Not posted.

---

Staying calm in a quiet house doesn't prove you're a calm guy. It might just mean it's been a quiet month. Plenty of us have had one of those stretches and decided, privately, that we'd finally got this patience thing down. Nobody's sick, work is fine, the kids are asleep by eight, and you catch yourself thinking you've turned into a pretty even-keeled guy. Call it fair-weather patience. There's nothing wrong with it, except that nobody has asked it to do anything yet.

Then the week shows up. The youngest gets the stomach bug at 2 a.m., and by breakfast the middle one has it too. The car starts making the noise, the one that sounds expensive. Your mom calls Wednesday, very cheerful, to say the doctor just wants a few more tests. Nobody in the house has slept more than four hours, and that includes both of you. The dishwasher has been full of clean dishes since Monday because neither of you has had ninety seconds to empty it. That week came from nowhere in particular. It landed on both of you at once, which is why it's the only week that tells you anything.

Epictetus was a Stoic teacher who spent his early life as a slave and his later years running a school. His short handbook on living opens with one plain line, in George Long's translation: "Of things some are in our power, and others are not." Put simply, some things belong to you and some never did. Then he sorts things into two lists. Yours is short. It holds what you think, what you want, what you avoid, and what you do. Other people's moods aren't on it. The body is on the other list, so the stomach bug was never yours to control, and the quiet month wasn't either. Patience that only shows up when everything outside you cooperates was built on the wrong list.

Finding out which kind of calm you have takes one bad week and a little attention. The next time something real lands, the tell is usually small. For a lot of men it's a sentence that starts with "Can everybody just." Or she asks whether anyone called the pharmacy, and you answer like she asked it to annoy you. She's on the same four hours you are. None of that makes you a bad husband. It means the patience was on loan from the quiet month, and the month wants it back. The other kind is plainer than you'd expect. You're tired and you're worried about your mom, and you still hear her question as a question. She's finding out the same thing about herself, by the way, in front of the same full dishwasher.

The car, for the record, is still making the noise.
