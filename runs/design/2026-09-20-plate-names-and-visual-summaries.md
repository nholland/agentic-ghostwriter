# Plate Names and Visual Summaries

*2026-09-20*

This is a reference for people looking at the plates cold, before reading the book. Fifteen plates: one per chapter for Chapters 1 through 12, plus a closing plate for each of the three Parts. Each entry gives the plate's name and a short description of what the image does visually — what the eye lands on, what the shapes argue against each other, and what a viewer would most likely say it means without any chapter behind it. Nothing here reveals more of a chapter than the plate itself already shows on the page. The three Part plates carry no title text of their own; they are named here by their Part.

## The Three-Second Window

The eye lands on one long horizontal bar with a short black block at the far left and a long empty white remainder running off to the right. The argument is proportional: the black piece is tiny next to the white, and it sits at the start, before everything else. Cold, a viewer says this is about how small the moment at the beginning is compared with everything that follows it, and that the small piece is the one that starts the rest.

*Chapter 1*

## The Mood Mirror

Two horizontal lines stacked one above the other, the same length, the same journey across — read as the same trip taken twice. In the top line the tick marks are all the same weight, so nothing interrupts the run; in the bottom line one tall tick cuts through the line and stands above and below it. A viewer reads this as: the same path, once with nothing in the middle and once with something planted in the middle, and the thing planted in the middle is what makes the second one different.

*Chapter 2*

## The Closed Door

Four stacked bars of identical length, filling with dark from the right, one step at a time down the page, until the last bar has only a sliver of white left. The eye reads the staircase of darkening before it reads any word: something is closing in four moves, and each move is bigger than the one before. Cold, a viewer calls this a gauge running out, or a door shutting a bit at a time until nearly nothing is left open.

*Chapter 3*

## The Hole Maker

Three stacked rows: a bar with five nails driven through it, then the same bar with five dots and no nails, then the same bar with ten dots. The eye goes to the nails first because they break the bar's outline, then discovers that removing them did not remove anything — the count of marks went up, not down. Cold, a viewer says this is about damage that stays after the thing that caused it is taken away, and that it keeps accumulating.

*Chapter 4*

## The Courage to Come Back

*(Naming note: the plate is titled "The courage to come back," while the chapter's distillation still names this mechanism "The Remaining Nails." The mismatch is open and unresolved — treat the drawn title as the working name.)*

Two panels, each with a tall bar at the left and a shorter bar at the right, the same setup twice. In the top panel the right bar is short and a dashed ghost outline floats above it showing the height it did not reach; in the bottom panel that same space is filled solid and the bar comes back up to the level of the one it started at. A viewer reads this as: go in this size, come out smaller — unless the missing piece gets put in, in which case you come out the size you went in.

*Chapter 5*

## Part I — The Steady River

A narrow vertical cleft running the whole height of the page, cut through horizontal strata lines, with a tiny ripple of water at the very bottom. The eye registers the enormous banked rock first and the thread of water last, which is the argument reversed into scale: the small persistent thing made the huge thing. Cold, a viewer says this is a canyon, and that whatever cut it took a very long time and never stopped.

*Part I closing plate*

## The Tally You Don't Read Aloud

Two bars, one above the other, the same length and the same position. The top one is solid black and heavy; the bottom one is an empty dashed outline. The visual argument is presence against invisibility — the same quantity exists twice, but only one of them can be seen. Cold, a viewer says: this thing is real and this thing is not visible, and they are the same thing.

*Chapter 6*

## The Private Tally

Two boxes side by side: the left one holds three legible written lines, the right one holds three blank dashed placeholder lines. Below both, running wider than either, a dense field of small scattered strokes covers the page. The eye reads the two boxes as a comparison and then notices the scatter is bigger than both of them put together — a viewer says the two lists disagree, and the real amount is the part in neither list.

*Chapter 7*

## The Tipping Scale

Two balance scales side by side. The left one has collapsed to one side under a pan full of small dots that have rained down onto it; the right one is level, with the same dots split between both pans. The eye goes straight to the tilt and reads the asymmetry as failure — cold, a viewer says small things collected on one side until it fell over, and that putting the same small things on both sides keeps it upright.

*Chapter 8*

## The Bottomless Yes

Three short solid arrows fire left to right, each from something given toward something expected. Beneath all three, one long dashed arrow runs the full width of the plate back the other way, pointing at empty space, and below that a small stack of ruled lines with the last one cut short. The eye reads the solid outgoing against the dashed return: everything went one way, nothing came back, and something is still being written down.

*Chapter 9*

## The Undecided Line

Two horizontal lines under identical downward-pointing arrows of pressure. The top line buckles into a V where the arrow hits it; the bottom line, with a solid dark block wedged under the same point, stays flat. This is the cleanest before-and-after on any plate: same load, same line, one supported and one not — a viewer says a thing holds only if something is put underneath it.

*Chapter 10*

## The Conversation She's Never Heard

Two boxes at the same size and height. The left is solid-bordered and packed with seven heavy dark bars stacked tight; the right is an empty dashed outline with nothing in it. A single arrow crosses the gap between them. The eye reads the density difference instantly — a full container beside an empty one — and a viewer says: all of this is over here, and none of it ever got across to there.

*Chapter 11*

## Part II — The Sturdy Oak

A tree's cross-section, filling the plate, drawn as forty-odd concentric rings around a single tiny dot at the centre. The eye follows the rings outward and finds two or three places where they crowd and kink — evidence of hard years absorbed into the shape without breaking it. Cold, a viewer says: this is a tree that has been through things and kept growing, and you can see the record of it in the wood.

*Part II closing plate*

## The Thing With No Deadline

Two horizontal timelines running to the same right-hand endpoint. The top is packed evenly with dark squares all the way across; the bottom has three squares bunched at the far left and then a long, conspicuous stretch of nothing before one hollow open square at the end. The eye is caught by the emptiness in the lower line — a viewer says something that used to happen stopped happening, nothing marked its absence, and the one open square is the chance to do it again.

*Chapter 12*

## Part III — The Warm Sun

Two banks of stacked horizontal lines that lie flat and closed at the bottom of the page and peel apart as they rise, opening a widening gap up the middle. Nothing forces the opening; the lines simply curve away from each other. Cold, a viewer says this is something opening — a book, a pair of curtains, a thing that was shut and is now parting from the bottom up.

*Part III closing plate*
