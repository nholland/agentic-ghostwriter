## Chapter 14: The Discipline of Enough
**Premise:** The comparison a man loses to is never a real rival — it's an imagined composite built from other people's best single traits, and no real wife, or real husband, can survive being measured against a highlight reel.
**The reader's takeaway:** He can name the specific mechanism that turns a stranger's one striking trait into apparent proof his wife isn't enough, and has a standard — contentment as a chosen, practiced discipline, not a feeling he's waiting to arrive — for what "enough" actually means in the marriage he already chose.
**Key points:**
1. The hedonic treadmill, applied to one specific marriage: the courtship-era intensity was never purely about her — it ran substantially on novelty and uncertainty, and its fading is universal, not a verdict on her, on him, or on the marriage; a man who doesn't know this mistakes ordinary brain chemistry for proof something is wrong
2. Miswanting and the comparison trap: attraction to a striking trait in someone else — a laugh, a look, an easy rapport — is a comparison between one spiking quality and his wife's entire, known, real person, complaints and bad days included; no whole person survives that comparison, including her, if she ran the same one on him
3. "Enough" as jurisdiction aimed at desire itself: the same governing principle used against outsourced mood (Chapter 2) and scorekeeping (Chapter 7), now aimed at wanting — he doesn't control the stray attraction crossing his mind, only whether he feeds it or names it
4. Proactive vs. reactive, and why this book leads with the first: most men only address contentment after the wandering eye or the resentment has already shown up (gratitude lists after the fact, "this is normal" self-talk, chasing novelty impulsively). The Stoic Husband treats it as a discipline practiced in advance — naming the coming lull before it arrives, planning real novelty before boredom goes looking for its own, and savoring as a standing habit rather than a rescue
**Central story/example:** A man on a work trip who can't stop thinking about a colleague's easy laugh and effortless confidence — and, that same week, a conversation with his wife where he realizes she could build an equally damning highlight reel about some other man if she let herself, and neither of them would survive that game, so neither of them should play it
**Stoic lesson / principle:** Contentment as chosen practice — Epictetus on desire aimed at what is present and real, not what is imagined; jurisdiction extended from mood and scorekeeping to desire itself
**Reader ah-ha:** "I wasn't comparing her to a person. I was comparing her to a highlight reel — and so could she, about me, if she let herself."
**Research burden:** Medium — Gilbert & Wilson on miswanting; Brickman & Campbell on the hedonic treadmill; Epictetus on desire and jurisdiction; `okf/frameworks/the-discipline-of-enough.md` and `okf/frameworks/the-hedonic-treadmill.md` as the evidentiary base
**Word count target:** 1,200–1,500 words
**Transition to Chapter 15:** Contentment with what's real quiets the comparison. But it doesn't resolve what happens when the most vulnerable form of wanting her gets a no. Chapter 15 takes on desire's hardest test case.
