## 1. Emmons and McCullough

Could not open the paper. All copies were blocked: Greater Good PDF (the ledger's `resource`), the UC Davis faculty PDF, DOI, PubMed. The only evidence available was search synthesis, which can locate a claim but not confirm it. It says: Studies 1–2 randomly assigned people to hassles, gratitude listing, and either neutral events (Study 1, weekly) or social comparison (Study 2, daily). Study 3 assigned people with neuromuscular disease to gratitude or a control. Gratitude groups showed higher well-being "across several, though not all" measures, and positive affect was the most robust result. That matches the ledger record (Researcher page-text, 2026-09-29), which adds: Study 2 gratitude beat hassles on positive affect but not downward social comparison, and negative affect did not differ.

**Defect flagged by search:** one search answer gave Study 1 participants "28% more" exercise and "half an hour" more sleep. I did not read either number in the paper, and the sleep finding may belong to Study 3. Do not use either number.

Candidate replacements. All are paraphrase. None is confirmed against the paper this session, so the author must check each one against the PDF. Ranked safest first:

## 2. Seneca, Letter 16

Source: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Moral_letters_to_Lucilius/Letter_16 (page credits translator Richard Mott Gummere). The Stoic Library page was blocked.

- Addressed to Lucilius: §1 opens "It is clear to you, I am sure, Lucilius, …". CONFIRMED.
- Epicurus credited in **§7**: "This also is a saying of Epicurus: “If you live according to nature, you will never be poor; if you live according to opinion, you will never be rich.”" (footnote: Frag. 201 Usener). CONFIRMED.
- Natural limits versus opinion: §8–9. CONFIRMED.

**Proposed correction (not applied):** Epicurus is credited with the *saying* that sets nature against opinion. The limited/limitless gloss is Seneca's own (§8–9). Suggested wording: "credits another philosopher, Epicurus, with a saying that sets nature against opinion."

**Stopping-point test:** this is fair to §9, which reads "consider whether it can stop at any definite point." Make it clearly Seneca's: "Seneca gives a test: can this desire stop at any definite point?"

## 3. The Seneca quote (Gummere, Wikisource, page-text, unverified)

Copied from the page:

- **Short (§9, 16 words):** "Natural desires are limited; but those which spring from false opinion can have no stopping-point."
  - Alternative short (§8, 10 words): "Nature’s wants are slight; the demands of opinion are boundless."
- **Longer (§9):** "Natural desires are limited; but those which spring from false opinion can have no stopping-point. The false has no limits. … when you would know whether that which you seek is based upon a natural or upon a misleading desire, consider whether it can stop at any definite point."

Wording that may need modernising: "spring from", "that which you seek", "stopping-point" (hyphenated). "Boundless" in §8 is fine. Any modernised version would be the author's own paraphrase, not Gummere, and must not appear in quotation marks attributed to Gummere. The "Recall your steps" and road/travel image belong to §9; the ledger advises keeping them out.
