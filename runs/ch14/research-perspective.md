## The short answer to the author's question

Yes, there are studies on this. The best-supported finding is close to what he
described, though not exactly the same.

1. **People feel grateful for a benefit when they see it as having cost the
   giver something, as meant for their good, and as valuable to them.**
   Psychologists call these "benefit appraisals." The line runs from Tesser,
   Gatewood and Driver (1968) to Wood et al. (2008), whose third study
   manipulated the appraisals and found that they shaped gratitude.
2. **These appraisals can be taught.** Froh et al. (2014) randomly assigned
   classrooms of children aged 8 to 11 to lessons on noticing a helper's
   intention, the cost to the helper and the benefit to themselves. The
   children who had the lessons became more grateful. This is the nearest
   research parallel to his children learning what dinner costs.
3. **In couples, gratitude follows what you notice, and a lot goes unnoticed.**
   In Visserman et al. (2019), sacrifices that went unnoticed produced no
   gratitude. Noticing a sacrifice, accurately or not, is what produced it. In
   Joel et al. (2013), seeing a partner's investments in the relationship
   predicted gratitude and then commitment. In Ross and Sicoly (1979), married
   partners recalled their own contributions to shared activities more readily
   than their spouse's.
4. **Taking things for granted is the normal default, not a character flaw.**
   On average, satisfaction rises around a good change and then drifts back
   toward where it was. Lucas et al. (2003) found this pattern around
   marriage, with large individual differences. Sheldon and Lyubomirsky
   (2012) propose that continued appreciation and variety slow the drift.
5. **The "grass is greener" pull is partly an error in imagining.** People
   judge an imagined alternative by its most visible feature (Schkade and
   Kahneman 1998, the focusing illusion). They predict their own enjoyment of
   an experience better from someone who has lived it than from a description
   of it (Gilbert et al. 2009). And they tend to like a choice they cannot
   reverse more than one they can (Gilbert and Ebert 2002). None of these
   studies is about marriage.

**What no study located here shows** (two gaps; see the end of this file):

- Doing your spouse's work yourself makes you more grateful to her.
- Men who leave for an imagined better partner find it is not better.

Treat both as the author's experience and judgment, not findings. In
particular, **"studies show the grass is not greener" is not a finding** and
must not be printed.
