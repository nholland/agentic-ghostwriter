## Chapter 14: The Discipline of Enough
**Premise:** A husband practices contentment by giving sustained attention and gratitude to the real person his wife becomes across years of change. He tends the marriage he has, so beauty, income, talent and passing comparisons do not become conditions on his love.
**The reader's takeaway:** He can practice enough through attention, specific appreciation, gratitude and care for the marriage he has. He can notice when an imagined alternative or an endlessly changing standard prevents him from valuing the woman beside him.
**Key points:**
1. Enough begins with where he places his attention. Familiarity can make the good in a shared life easy to overlook. Notice the actual person and life in front of him before an imagined alternative sets the standard.
2. Appreciation grows specific. Notice qualities, acts and shared history he has begun to treat as guaranteed. Name something he values about her, and enjoy qualities that belong to her even when they do nothing for him. Specific appreciation is the core practice; reflecting on how they might never have met is optional supporting material, not a required exercise.
3. Watering is active love. Appreciation appears in attention, expressed gratitude and care on ordinary days, before dissatisfaction makes it urgent. Offer it freely without requiring her response to validate it. Research can support modest claims about practicing gratitude; it does not prove that gratitude creates a contented marriage or cures disconnection.
4. Comparison and moving standards can obstruct contentment. Recognize when another person's striking trait becomes a new condition on affection. Keep a brief wisdom boundary: care for one's own health and raise genuine concerns, without making a wife's changing body, income or gifts the measure of her worth. No fixed decision test is commissioned.
**Central story/example:** The author's real account of his changing wife, drawing selectively on supplied testimony: she is a light sleeper who hears sounds at night and wakes him to check them; a morning person who gets the household up even when they are tired; frugal, bickering over discarding small things and retrieving things from the trash; reluctant to show the new gray hairs he loves; formerly a high-paid executive, now on sabbatical as a full-time mother with a green thumb. Both their bodies have changed. Use supplied details and ongoing testimony; do not invent dialogue, chronology, a conversion scene or an author mistake. The unconfirmed work-trip story is retired from this chapter's requirements.
**Stoic lesson / principle:** Contentment as a chosen practice of attention and appreciation. Marcus Aurelius, Meditations VI.48, offers the principal positive anchor: recalling the virtues of people nearby. Seneca Letter 16.7–9 may supply a brief supporting account of desires without a stopping point, with Epicurus credited where Seneca credits him. Applying either text to marriage is the author's application, not an ancient marriage experiment.
**Reader ah-ha:** "I can choose to notice, appreciate and tend the life I already share with her, and love who she is becoming."
