## Essential findings (these would change the verdict)

### E1. The hunger-sauce passage reads as a backhanded compliment about her cooking, and the reader has to work out the link himself
**Raised by:** Skeptic, with Eric and Paul agreeing. **Location:** "The woman you love now", paragraph 4.

> "An older proverb says hunger is the best sauce. Xenophon puts it in Socrates' mouth, and Seneca lived it: with only poor bread on hand, he waited until hunger made it taste good (*Letters* 123). Anything she makes is enough for me."

Here is the order a first-time reader gets: (1) breakfast out with no kids, (2) she says the best meals are the ones you don't cook, (3) hunger makes poor bread taste good, (4) "Anything she makes is enough for me." Put next to each other, (3) and (4) say her food is the poor bread, and he has lowered the bar until it tastes fine. That is the opposite of what the author means, and it lands in the chapter called *She Is Enough*. A defensive man takes away "you put up with it." A man who reads passages aloud to his wife will not read this one aloud.

There is also a logic gap. The paragraph opens on seeing her clearly when he's rested and has time with her as a friend. Then it turns into food philosophy without saying how the two connect. The point the author wants is probably this: the eater's state makes the meal, the same way his attention picked "hoarder" or "thrifty". The prose never says that, so the reader has to infer it. The voice spec's "never make the reader infer a thing you could name" covers exactly this case.

**Cost to fix:** low. One bridging sentence, in the author's words, saying what the proverb means for him and her. For example, that his own state, rested and glad of her company, is the seasoning, not what's on the plate. Or move "Anything she makes is enough for me" so it no longer follows "poor bread." Both his line and his wife's line can stay. Only the order and the missing link need work. The close's "Slow down enough to taste them" depends on this image, so whatever changes here has to keep that working.

### E2. Two endings: an image close, then a moral after it
**Raised by:** Tension Reader and Skeptic. Beta reader Eric partly disagrees (see below). **Location:** the last two paragraphs.

> "They don't feel like much while you're in them. They are the life you chose. Slow down enough to taste them."
>
> "Thankfulness is how you keep wanting what you already have."

"Slow down enough to taste them" is the chapter's own image, and it already brings back the breakfast and the thing she saved. The final line then states the lesson in the abstract. The voice spec bans both moves: "Never wrap up with a moral" (the close should be "an image the author could have arrived at himself") and "Never write two endings" (the sentence after a close may not restate what it just landed). Technically the final line is one sentence with nothing after it, so a mechanical one-sentence check would pass it. Read as a reader, though, the chapter ends twice.

A second problem: the line sounds like the familiar aphorism "happiness is wanting what you have." Eric has "low tolerance for vague inspiration" (02-audience), and he will hear a fridge magnet. Nothing marks the line as the author's own.

**Where personas disagree, unaveraged:** Eric's beta read rates the line as the most quotable sentence in the chapter, the one he would screenshot or text a friend. It also ties back to Seneca: thankfulness gives wanting a stopping point. The spec records it as the Reader ah-ha. The Tension Reader's view is that the chapter has already landed by then and the line explains it after the fact. Both are true. That disagreement is the finding.

**Cost to fix:** low, and it's the author's choice. Either end on "Slow down enough to taste them" and move the thankfulness line earlier, for example into "Let appreciation become care" where it can do argumentative work, or keep the thankfulness line as the close and cut "Slow down enough to taste them." Either way there should be one ending. If he keeps the moral, the spec's ah-ha stands as written. If he keeps the image, the spec's ah-ha line changes.

### E3. The distillation claims something the chapter doesn't say
**Raised by:** Continuity Editor (within the chapter). **Location:** `distillation.md`, summary paragraph.

> "Gratitude leaves room for honest concerns and care for your own health."

The prose never mentions his own health. The spec says so too: "Care for his own health, which the old spec listed and the distillation still carries, is no longer in the prose." The distillation lands with the chapter. A reader of the distillation or the practice guide would get a promise the chapter never makes.

**Cost to fix:** trivial. Cut "and care for your own health." The rest of the distillation matches the prose, and the Conversation sentence describes what the chapter actually does.

## Optional findings (worth the author's eye, would not by themselves change the verdict)

**O1. Seneca's test is only used against his wants. Skeptic, strongest objection.**
The objection at full strength: *"Telling a man his wife 'is enough' teaches him to lower his expectations and stay quiet. That breeds resentment and withdrawal, which this book fights everywhere else. And Seneca's own test cuts both ways. Natural desires are limited and **legitimate**. A man who wants affection, closeness or partnership and doesn't get it isn't chasing opinion. He has a need that has a stopping point. The chapter only uses the test to discredit wants."*
Where the chapter answers it, and answers it well: "You can be glad she's your wife and still feel hurt when you reach for her and hear no. What you decide is how to treat her while you feel it." Also: "Contentment doesn't replace a conversation that needs to happen... That takes judgment." Where it doesn't: the Seneca paragraph ("Seneca's test is to ask whether the thing you want can stop at any definite point.") never admits that some of what he wants from her passes the test. Paul, whose need is real, hears that every want is suspect. **Cost:** one sentence in the Seneca paragraph granting that a want with a stopping point is real and worth saying out loud. This does not add a decision test, which the author ruled out.

**O2. "Her income has changed" sits in a list a defensive reader could take for depreciation. Skeptic.**
> "Her income has changed. Her body has changed. Mine has too. When she's working in the garden in shorts and a T-shirt, I find her incredibly attractive, and that's enough."
The spec intends this: income and beauty shouldn't become conditions. But the short flat sentences read like a list of losses, and "that's enough" can sound like "sufficient." The man most likely to misread it is the one who already keeps that list. "Mine has too" helps. **Cost:** low. One clause saying her worth to him was never her paycheck would close it, if the author wants that. Otherwise leave it as his own words.

**O3. "When either of you notices it, you can say so kindly" doesn't say what is noticed, and it gives a defensive man an opening. Skeptic, with Paul.**
> "Somewhere there's a man who earns more, another who's more patient, another who makes her laugh. You'd want her to remember the whole of you, and she'd want the same from you. When either of you notices it, you can say so kindly."
Notices what? Comparison in himself, or in her? Read the second way, it gives him permission to tell his wife she's comparing him. That is the "what about her?" objection (02-audience, #2), handed to him when the book's answer is jurisdiction. Add "Your wife may do it too" two paragraphs earlier, and her comparing gets two mentions in a chapter about his attention. **Cost:** low. Name the thing noticed, and cut either "Your wife may do it too" or the second mention.

**O4. "In a 2003 study ... Across three studies" contradicts itself inside one paragraph. Continuity, with Eric.**
> "In a 2003 study, psychologists Robert Emmons and Michael McCullough had people write down what they were grateful for. Across three studies, ..."
Eric is evidence-minded and will notice the switch from one to three. **Cost:** one word ("paper" or "report"). I did not check the finding's wording against the source, which is out of scope. The spec notes that "most clearly a better mood" is still search-level only.

**O5. The tension peaks halfway through, then levels off into instructions. Tension Reader.**
The peak is the Seneca question: "If your wife met today's condition, would you let yourself enjoy her? Or would you find the next thing she lacks?" That comes at about 45%. After it, Marcus (three short paragraphs) names the practice, and "Practice before you need it" opens on a study rather than a moment. That's roughly 450 words of instruction with nothing at stake. The one place it lifts again is the author's own admission: "I think a lot of it can land as hollow, like an 'I love you' said with nothing behind it." That admission is buried in the fourth paragraph of the practice section. The chapter's hardest honest line, "...still feel hurt when you reach for her and hear no," sits in a caveat paragraph that starts with an awkward "Nor." These lines carry the chapter's second high point. Within the settled structure, the cheap lever is weight, not order: give those two lines their own room. Also, the author's opening mistake (hoarder vs thrifty) never comes back as practice, except as an image in the close. The reader never sees him use the better word with her.

**O6. "Let appreciation become care" is the mechanism, and it's the thinnest section. Beta reader, Eric.**
> "That can look like a kinder answer, a sincere compliment, or time spent enjoying her company."
The Conversation sentence promises that appreciation changes how he cares for her. The care examples are general, and "time spent enjoying her company" loops back to appreciation. Eric wants something he can do on Tuesday. **Cost:** moderate. One concrete care act from the author's own life would anchor it. No invention: it has to be something he supplies.

**O7. Seneca and Xenophon are named before anyone says who they are. Continuity.**
The first mention of Seneca is bare ("Seneca lived it"). His introduction, "a Roman Stoic philosopher writing to his friend Lucilius," comes a section later. Xenophon is never introduced, and 02-audience doesn't list him as someone the reader knows. **Cost:** low. Move the Seneca gloss to the first mention and either gloss Xenophon in three words or drop him.

**O8. Small lines that make the reader rebuild who did what.**
- "Someone else catches your eye. But the one thing you've noticed becomes a new condition..." The "But" contrasts with nothing.
- "Stay in the conversation because you like talking to her." Which conversation?
- "Spouses compare, husbands and wives both, often without noticing." This reads as a universal claim. "A lot of spouses" would narrow it.
- "A wife who has stepped back from a career may miss feeling important..." This generalizes from his wife to the reader's, and it doesn't fit a reader whose wife works.

**O9. Two image families. Tension Reader, reading only, not counted.**
The grass, fence and water image is set up early ("Life on the other side of the fence can look better") and pays off in the last section. Hunger, sauce and taste is a second family, and it's the one the close uses. The voice spec allows one anchor image. I didn't count the family words. The Conformance Checker should count them, and the author may decide the second family is earned.

## Beta reader summaries

**Eric (primary).** Feels addressed in the first paragraph ("Ask what you appreciate about her today, and you might need a minute"). Respects the Seneca test, because it is rigor, not inspiration. Feels slightly lectured in the practice section. Could stall at the hunger paragraph (E1). Would tell a friend: *"There's a line that asks whether, if your wife met today's condition, you'd actually let yourself enjoy her. That stuck."*

**Paul (secondary).** For the first half he may feel the chapter belongs to a man whose marriage is fine: the gray hairs, the garden, breakfast with no kids. The book reaches him in three places: "If nothing comes to mind, you haven't failed," "you don't have to pretend the hurt disappeared," and "hear no." He feels most accused by nothing in particular, but O1 is the place he would argue back. Would tell a friend: *"It's a gratitude chapter. Good if you're not in a hole. It does admit she might say no."*

## Closing line verdict
"Thankfulness is how you keep wanting what you already have" is a good sentence, and it's in the wrong place: after the image close, as a moral, and it sounds like a known aphorism. It earns a place in the chapter but not as the last word, unless "Slow down enough to taste them" goes. See E2. That's the author's choice.
