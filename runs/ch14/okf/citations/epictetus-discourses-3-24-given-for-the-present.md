# Source finding

When kissing one's child, brother or friend, Epictetus counsels not giving full license to the impression; remember the one you love is mortal and what you love is not your own, given for the present and not for all time, like a fig or grapes in their season; to wish for it out of season is like wanting a fig in winter. When delighted with a thing, place the contrary appearance before yourself; he defends saying to a child 'to-morrow you will die' as useful, not ill-omened. Marcus repeats the kissing saying at Meditations XI.34 (Long), citing iii.24.88.

# Limits

The passage's center is mortality, which Chapter 27 owns (outline 2026-10-02). In a Sun-arc chapter on warmth the death line reads as morbid and risks the 'Stoicism as armor' failure. At most, the 'given for the present' idea fits Ch14's perspective theme, and only on the author's choice. Could later fill the canonical gap marcus-aurelius-impermanence-and-gratitude.md for Ch27.
