# Chapter 12: Romance Is a Discipline

There's a version of you that used to plan things. You know roughly when that stopped, and you've probably filed it under the phase of life you're in. Filing it there is the mistake. You made that decision a few hundred times, and you never once noticed you were making one.

---

**The second piece of advice.** My grandfather gave me two things worth keeping about marriage. You've already had the one about giving sixty and expecting forty. The other one came when I first got married, and it was harder to take: "Put your wife first. Focus on her because your kids will leave you."

That came off as harsh. It still does. You're supposed to say your family is everything. You're not supposed to rank the people inside it. Here was an old man ranking them out loud to a groom. And this is the same man whose temper I inherited. He never had a gap between what he felt and what came out of his mouth. He got plenty wrong. He got this right.

What I think he was saying is simpler than it sounded. When you pick a partner, you treat that person like a top priority. You keep treating her that way after the house fills up with other people who need you more loudly. The kids grow and go. That's the job. She's the one still sitting across from you when they do. And when you stop treating her like a top priority, that's when the cracks start.

---

**The first thing cut.** It makes sense that you stopped, and I'd rather say why than pretend you got lazy.

Romance takes real effort, and effort comes out of a supply that runs down. The more life takes out of you, the less you have left over for her. So when a week gets heavy, something gets cut. And the thing that goes first is never the mortgage, or the deadline, or the kid's game at eight on a Saturday. Those shout. They have dates attached and other people watching. Romance has neither. Nobody calls you at work because you haven't surprised your wife since March. So it goes first, quietly, every time, and you never catch the decision because you never sat down and made one.

You already run the rest of your life on purpose. You decide what you train and what you take on at work. You decide what your temper is allowed to do in a kitchen at seven on a weeknight. Discipline is the word you'd use for all of it, and you'd use it accurately. This is the one corner of your life where you never decided anything. It's also the corner your wife reads to find out whether she's still wanted.

Marcus Aurelius was a Roman emperor who wrote notes to himself at night, mostly about how to be a decent man in the morning. He'd read an older philosopher, Democritus, who said that if you want to be untroubled, do few things. Marcus thought that was close but not right, and fixed it: do the necessary things. Those are two different lists, and the second one is worse to write, because it makes you say out loud what actually counts.

In my own marriage, romance ebbs and flows. We've heard the same advice you've heard, that you should get out on a date once a week. We have gone weeks and sometimes months without going on a date.

---

**Her currency, not yours.** Romance is trying to win favor with your wife. It isn't about you, it's about her, and some of it is a little mystery, a little surprise, a little intrigue. Which means the whole thing runs on study. You can't win favor with a person you quit learning.

You default to the golden rule here without ever deciding to. Treat her the way you'd want to be treated. A communication researcher named Milton Bennett named the problem with that back in 1979. The golden rule smuggles in an assumption: the other person is basically like you, and wants what you'd want. What he proposed instead sometimes gets called the platinum rule. Treat her the way she wants to be treated. It's a harder job, because first you have to find out what that is.

Say the thing in your kitchen has been broken since spring and you give it a Saturday. Six hours, a trip out for the part, two skinned knuckles. If someone had done that for you, you'd have been floored. She says thank you, and she means it, and it's flat. You carry that flatness around for three days, building a quiet case that she's ungrateful.

She isn't ungrateful. The effort was real and the care underneath it was real, and it arrived in a form she doesn't read as love. What she reads is you asking about the thing she's been dreading at work. Then asking again on Thursday, because you remembered there was a Thursday. You're both right about what you experienced. Only one of you can see why it didn't land.

This is also why romance at year ten looks smaller than most men expect it to. The trip and the party are rarely it. What lands is the detail you kept and the question you came back to. It's the small thing that says I still see you, instead of I planned something impressive. A gesture gets arranged once. Attention doesn't get to stop.

It runs the other direction too. When love shows up in a form that doesn't land for you, the reflex is to overlook it. Or worse, to make a joke of it in front of someone. That's an insulting thing to do to a person who tried. Effort in the wrong language is still effort, and you owe it an honest read. None of it works as a transaction, either. If you're doing it to collect something later that night, she'll know. What she'll see is a man performing romance at her rather than giving it.

---

**What it costs.** A wife who stops feeling wanted and valued doesn't file a complaint about it.

Researchers who followed couples over time keep finding the same shape. Partners who feel appreciated tend to be more appreciative back, and more responsive to what the other one needs. They also tend to be more committed, and more likely to still be together years later. It travels as a loop. Which means it turns the other way just as easily, and when it does, nobody announces it. First there's a little more distance in the room than there used to be. Then resentment. That's just the ordinary word for what settles in when someone gives more than she gets for long enough to stop expecting better.

If you're a bare minimum partner, you get bare minimum in return. That's not her being petty. That's what anyone does after a few years of being somebody's leftover time.

Romance is as much an art as it is a science. It works like any muscle: the more you use it, the better you get at it. The reverse is the part nobody warns you about. Leave it alone long enough and it atrophies. Then the first honest attempt feels ridiculous, and expensive, and like something other men were just born knowing how to do.

---

She was never waiting on the feeling: she's been waiting to be the necessary thing, and that's a muscle you can start using tonight.
