## 1. What the chapter is

**Premise.** Romance is the first thing a man's energy budget cuts when life
gets expensive, and the cut is invisible to him and obvious to her. It is not a
feeling to wait for. It is a priority to defend.

**Mechanism, in the author's words:**

> "My sense is that I've noticed that it takes a lot of effort and because life
> can be busy and stressful, this is the first barometer that I try to think
> about when I'm thinking about my own romantic side. **The more life takes a
> toll on you, the less energy you have to invest in your partner.**"

This is not a man waiting for a mood. It is a man whose budget is
over-committed, spending his effort where it shows a faster return. The
intervention is about priority and cost, not about feeling. Feeling-follows-
practice tells him to act anyway; energy-allocation tells him romance is
competing with work, kids and exhaustion, and losing.

**The reader's takeaway.** He can see that romance didn't fade, it got cut —
by him, without a decision, every week, in favour of things that shout louder.
And he knows the cut is not fixed by a grand gesture but by delivering care in
the form she actually reads as care.

**Reader ah-ha (proposed, not ruled).** "I've been loving her in my language
and calling it effort."

## 4. The sharp idea — platinum over golden

**AUTHOR RULING 2.** *"Let's go platinum. I like that."*

**The claim:** treat her the way **she** wants to be treated, not the way you
would want to be treated.

**Why it is sharp rather than obvious:** the golden rule is what a decent man
defaults to, and the default is the failure. He puts in real effort, delivered
in his own currency, and it does not land. He gets no credit; she gets no care;
both of them are correct about their own experience. That is the chapter's most
useful single paragraph and it should be written for the man who has been doing
the effort, not for the man who has been doing nothing.

**It binds both people.** The author's own receiving-side observation, from
round 1:

> "When you get love in a way that doesn't resonate with you, a natural response
> is to overlook it or basically not see the value. If someone throws it in your
> face or even does something terrible like downplay it, that's quite insulting
> because the person is putting in effort. You just might not receive it that
> way."

So: the giver owes her the study of her currency; the receiver owes effort
spent in the wrong currency an honest reading. Write both halves. The second
half is what keeps the chapter from reading as a list of demands on him, which
is `02-audience.md` objection 2 ("this puts all the responsibility on me").

**Attribution — required.** "The platinum rule" is an existing coinage. Milton
J. Bennett coined it in 1979, in a communication-studies chapter arguing the
Golden Rule hides an assumption that other people are like you. Name him, once,
in the book's conversational register — something of the shape *"a communication
researcher named Milton Bennett gave this a name back in 1979."* Do not imply
the author coined it.
Concept: `runs/ch12/okf/citations/bennett-1979-platinum-rule.md` (status
`verifiable`).

## 5. The five love languages — DO NOT BUILD ON IT

The author leaned on this in round 1 as a load-bearing pillar: *"You have to
first understand the five love languages of your partner: how do they give love
and how do they receive love?"*

**It does not hold empirically. This was established independently this session,
not assumed.** Chapman's typology came out of a pastoral counselling practice,
not a study, and its three central assumptions fail when tested:

1. **Everyone has one primary love language.** Not supported. People endorse all
   five as meaningful ways to give and receive love.
2. **There are five, and they are distinct.** Not supported. The categories
   overlap substantially. The original sample was narrow (white, religious,
   mixed-gender, traditional couples).
3. **Matching your partner's language improves the relationship.** Not
   supported. No consistent association with satisfaction either way. All
   expressions of love are associated with higher satisfaction regardless of
   anyone's stated primary language.

Sources, both shadow concepts:
- `runs/ch12/okf/citations/impett-park-muise-2024-love-languages-evaluated.md`
  — Impett, Park & Muise (2024), *Current Directions in Psychological Science*
  33, 87–92. The review. Status `verifiable`.
- `runs/ch12/okf/citations/bunt-hazelwood-2017-love-languages-self-regulation.md`
  — Bunt & Hazelwood (2017), *Personal Relationships* 24(2), 280–290. An
  independent empirical test of assumption 3; found alignment made little
  difference and self-regulation did. Status `verifiable`, small sample.
- `runs/ch12/okf/citations/chapman-1992-five-love-languages.md` — the source
  being evaluated. Status `unverified`, tagged do-not-present-as-research.

**The idea the author actually means survives. The framework does not.**

What survives: people differ in which expressions of care register for them,
and care delivered in a form a partner does not read as care can fail to land
even when the effort behind it was real. The construct relationship science
uses for this is **perceived partner responsiveness** — whether she comes away
believing you understand what matters to her, value it, and acted on it. It is
about accuracy of attention, not format of delivery. Impett and colleagues
point directly at it as the replacement.

**What the Ghostwriter does with this:**

- Write the idea. Do not write the framework.
- **Do not name "the five love languages" in the chapter as a model.** If the
  chapter names it at all, it names it as a popular idea and says plainly what
  holds and what does not, in one clause, without making the chapter about
  debunking. `02-audience.md`: Eric has a well-calibrated bullshit detector and
  will already half-know this. Quietly not using it is stronger than attacking
  it, and it costs no words the chapter has to spare.
- **Do not attribute the responsiveness idea to a named researcher.** Its
  primary is not pinned. See §9, open gap G-1. `04-archetype.md` sets citation
  density low, so the chapter can carry this idea in plain language with no
  citation at all, and should.
- **Do not import the balanced-diet metaphor** from the Impett paper.
  `01-voice.md` allows one concrete image per chapter and this chapter's image
  is already chosen (§10). A food metaphor on top of it is metaphor stacking.

## 6. The consequence chain — which link is weak

The author asserts, as certainty: partner does not feel wanted/valued/loved/
cherished → cracks → resentment → infidelity. Here is what each link can carry.

**Link 1 — not feeling valued → deterioration. SUPPORTED.** Gordon, Impett,
Kogan, Oveis & Keltner (2012), *JPSP* 103(2), 257–274, is the paper. Feeling
appreciated by your partner travels with being more appreciative of them, which
travels with being more responsive to their needs, more committed, and more
likely to still be together later. It is a loop, not a one-way arrow, which is
why the author's receiving-side observation (§4) belongs in the same chapter.
Concept: `runs/ch12/okf/citations/gordon-et-al-2012-gratitude-relationship-maintenance.md`.

> **Language constraint.** The designs are correlational and longitudinal, not
> experimental. Write what travels with what, not what causes what. "Couples
> where each person feels appreciated tend to stay together" is honest.
> "Feeling appreciated keeps couples together" is not.

**Link 2 — deterioration → resentment. PLAIN ENGLISH, NOT A FINDING.**
"Resentment" is not a measured construct in this literature. Nothing was found
that licenses it as a research claim. It is fine as ordinary description of
something readers recognise, and the book already owns resentment as its own
territory (Ch6, Ch7, Ch8). Write it as observation. Do not dress it as evidence.

**Link 3 — resentment → infidelity. THIS IS THE WEAK LINK. The chapter must
not state it as the author stated it.**

The nearest real evidence is Selterman, Garcia & Tsapelas (2019), *The Journal
of Sex Research* 56(3), 273–286: 495 self-reported infidelities sort into eight
distinct motivations, neglect among them. That is retrospective self-reported
motive, from an internet convenience sample skewed young, not married-specific.
It establishes that **people cite neglect**. It establishes nothing about
whether neglect produces infidelity, or how often, or for whom.
Concept: `runs/ch12/okf/citations/selterman-2019-infidelity-motivations.md`.

Working against a deterministic reading, and already in the book's own bundle:
`okf/citations/infidelity-motivation-gendered-split.md` records that a large
share of people who have affairs rate their marriage happy. The bundle's own
note says: *"Do NOT present this as a deterministic 'sexless marriage causes
infidelity' claim even once sourced — the complicating finding argues
explicitly against that framing."* The same restriction applies here.

**The ruling for the Ghostwriter.** State link 1 with confidence. Write link 2
as plain observation. For link 3, either:

- **(a) Cut infidelity from the chain entirely.** Recommended. The chapter is
  1,000–1,300 words, Ch22 owns betrayal as a subject, and the chain lands
  harder without a claim the reader can dismiss. "Cracks" and "resentment" are
  enough weight. Or
- **(b) Name it as one of several things it can lead to, never as where it
  leads.** No number, no rate, no named study, no implied inevitability.

Under no framing does this chapter print a statistic about infidelity.

> This is a correction to the author's own stated assertion. Per CLAUDE.md
> Rule 4 the repair is to bring the claim down to what the evidence supports,
> not to halt. Raised as an inbox item rather than resolved silently: see §9.

## 10. Structure, voice and shape — the Ghostwriter's constraints

**Word target: 1,000–1,300.** This is one of the book's short chapters. Ch10
and Ch11 run 2,400–2,800; this one does not. Cut early.

**Opening type: type 3, callback** (`04-archetype.md`). Two reasons that agree:
Ch11 opened with type 1 (direct address into the reader's experience) and the
archetype forbids repeating an approach in consecutive chapters; and the
grandfather is already known to the reader, which makes callback the honest
move rather than a device. Do **not** use the bounded-paragraph opening format
(`---` dividers around a short opening paragraph); Ch10 and Ch11 both open in
plain prose, but the format plus an explicit callback in the same chapter is
flagged in the archetype as a compounding echo.

**Orientation before the scene.** 1–3 sentences telling the reader what he is
about to realise, before the grandfather appears. A compass, not a
curtain-raiser.

**Beat structure.** 3–5 bold-labelled beats between the opening and the close,
`---` dividers between them. Labels are evocative and one to four words
("The first thing cut." not "Key Point 1"). A shape that fits the word count:

1. **The callback and the line.** The grandfather, the advice, the harshness,
   what it actually meant.
2. **The first thing cut.** The mechanism. Romance isn't fading, it's being
   cut, and he never decided to cut it. The author's own admission goes here or
   at the close — weeks and sometimes months. Marcus and the necessary things.
3. **Her currency, not yours.** Platinum over golden. Bennett named. The man
   doing real effort in the wrong language. Both halves: the giver's job and
   the receiver's.
4. **What it costs.** Feeling unvalued, cracks, resentment. Calibrated per §6.
   "If you're a bare minimum partner, you get bare minimum in return" lands
   here.
5. **Close.** One sentence, the author's own, on the chapter's image. Nothing
   after it.

**The anchor image: the muscle.** It is the author's own — *"it's like any
muscle: the more you use it, the more you get good at it"* — it is used zero
times in the manuscript, and it resolves the mechanism into something a tired
man can act on: the reason it feels expensive is that he hasn't done it in
months, and it gets cheaper with use. Cap the word family (*muscle, use,
strong, weak, atrophy, exercise*) at roughly **3 total mentions** and count
them literally; `01-voice.md` treats that as a counted rule. Do not also bring
in a budget image (R-2), a diet image (§5), or a sun image (Part-level, §8).

> *Researcher's note, offered as a read rather than a ruling: the muscle line
> and the energy-allocation mechanism are not in conflict, they are diagnosis
> and prescription. The mechanism explains why romance stops; the muscle
> explains why restarting feels so expensive and why it stops being expensive.
> It does sit close to the superseded askesis framing, so watch that the
> chapter does not drift back into "practice until the feeling comes." The
