# Source search: the boxer image ("you can't be a boxer without being punched in the face")

*Fact-Checker (gw-factchecker), 2026-10-04 18:52. Apparatus only. Nothing under
`books/` was edited (Rule 8). Nothing here is `verified`; only the author can set
that, against his own copy. Every quotation below was inserted by script from the
fetched files (`extract.py` → `quotes.json` in the session scratchpad), not retyped.
Whitespace was normalised. Scan-page markers (`{n}`, `{Page:…}`) and footnote calls
(`[ n ]`) were stripped, and each one is recorded in the locator.*

## Verdict

**A real Seneca source exists, and it really is a boxer getting punched in the
face. But the wording the author likes is not Seneca's. It is a compression.**

- **Best candidate: Seneca, *Letters* 13.1–3 (*On groundless fears*), in Richard
  Gummere's translation.** Gummere is the house edition for the Letters
  (`06-sources.md`). There, the prizefighter who "has never been beaten black and
  blue" cannot go confidently into the fight. The one who can is the man "who has
  felt his teeth rattle beneath his opponent's fist" and, "as often as he falls,
  rises again". This is the image, in Seneca, in the house translation.
- **The exact sentence "you can't be a boxer without being punched in the face"
  exists in none of the pages I read.** Seneca's point is narrower: no fighter can
  *trust himself* until he has been hit and got back up. It is not that you cannot
  *be* a boxer without it. The retired draft's sentences ("character can only be
  verified by what it survives", "the wrestler who has never been thrown doesn't
  know if he can get back up") are not Seneca's wording either. They paraphrase
  Letter 13.2 together with *Of Providence* IV.
- **The old draft got the attribution right and the source wrong.** The image is
  Seneca's, but in Letter 13. It is not in *De Providentia*, which has no boxer.
  *De Providentia* supplies the *idea* in its plainest form: "a man cannot know
  himself without a trial" (IV).
- **Epictetus has the nearest companion image.** In *Discourses* I.24 (Long 1877),
  God, "like a trainer of wrestlers, has matched you with a rough young man".
  It is about difficulty *showing* what a man is. Nobody gets hit in it.
  *Enchiridion* 29 is about counting the cost before you enter a contest, which is
  a different point.
- **Marcus does not support it.** *Meditations* VI.20 has a man struck on the head
  in the gymnasium, but the lesson is to hold no grudge, not that being struck
  tests you.

**Nearest honest paraphrase** (the author's own words, no quotation marks, with
Seneca credited for the idea):

> Seneca said it about prizefighters. A fighter who's never been beaten black and
> blue can't walk into the ring sure of himself. The one who can is the man who's
> seen his own blood, been knocked down, and got back up every time.
> (*Letters* 13)

The short form, "you can't be a boxer without being punched in the face", can stay
**as the author's own line**. It must not go in quotation marks or be
presented as Seneca's sentence. If it is quoted, quote Gummere's words exactly, as
below, and name Gummere.

## Candidates, in order of fit

### 1. Seneca, *Letters* 13.1–3: BEST. Supports the boxer image directly

- **Work:** *Ad Lucilium Epistulae Morales*, Letter 13, "On groundless fears",
  §§1–3.
- **Translator and edition:** Richard Mott Gummere, Loeb Classical Library, vol. 1
  (1917, per Wikisource's edition header: "A Loeb Classical Library edition;
  volume 1 published 1917"). I did not read the title-page imprint itself.
- **Page read:** https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Moral_letters_to_Lucilius/Letter_13
  (sha256 `4bceeca8…ee31def6`). This is a proofread transcription keyed to scans of
  the printed volume (`Index:Ad Lucilium epistulae morales, volume 1.djvu`).
  Proofreading level 3, "proofread": checked by one proofreader, not the two needed
  for "validated". Printed pages: §§1–2 begin on **p. 73** (scan 97); §2 continues
  from "been tripped" on **p. 75** (scan 99), where §3 also falls.
- **Wording on the page:**
  - §1: "For our powers can never inspire in us implicit faith in ourselves except when many difficulties have confronted us on this side and on that, and have occasionally even come to close quarters with us."
  - §2: "This is the touchstone of such a spirit; no prizefighter can go with high spirits into the strife if he has never been beaten black and blue; the only contestant who can confidently enter the lists is the man who has seen his own blood, who has felt his teeth rattle beneath his opponent’s fist, who has been tripped and felt the full force of his adversary’s charge, who has been downed in body but not in spirit, one who, as often as he falls, rises again with greater defiance than ever." *(the p. 73→75 page break falls between "who has" and
    "been tripped")*
  - §3: "So then, to keep up my figure, Fortune has often in the past got the upper hand of you, and yet you have not surrendered, but have leaped up and stood your ground still more eagerly. For manliness gains much strength by being challenged;"
- **Fit:** direct. It is a boxer, he is struck in the face ("teeth rattle beneath
  his opponent's fist"), and the claim is that only the man who has been hit and
  got up can trust himself. §3 turns it on Lucilius: "to keep up my figure,
  Fortune has often in the past got the upper hand of you". §2 also covers the
  wrestler half of the old draft ("tripped … as often as he falls, rises again").
  **Caveat on fit:** the claim is about *confidence*, about knowing you can take it.
  It is not that you cannot be a fighter at all. And the letter's real subject is
  fear: §4 goes straight on to "we suffer more often in imagination than in
  reality". That fits a "sea legs" theme well.
- **Recommended axes, if it becomes a concept:**
  `quote_form: verbatim` (if printed in quotes; otherwise `paraphrase`),
  `evidence_source: page-text`, `status: verifiable`.
  Reasons: I read a proofread transcription of the 1917 Loeb, page by page, with
  the printed page numbers. **I did not see the scan image itself.**
  `thumb.wikimedia.org` and `upload.wikimedia.org` are blocked (403, policy), and I
  did not route around them. If the author's bar for `verifiable` is the page
  image, not a transcription of it, hold this at `unverified` until a session
  with image access, or until he checks his copy.
- **Single-transcription caveat:** a second Gummere text (archive.org item
  `adluciliumepistu01sene`) is catalogued, but its download node is blocked
  (`dn790009.ca.archive.org`, 403), so I could not cross-check it. Web search shows
  the same wording circulating (Goodreads, Daily Stoic), but search cannot transcribe a

- **Latin (Gummere's Loeb text, Perseus `2007.01.0080`): not read.** The Perseus

### 2. Seneca, *De Providentia* (*Of Providence*): supports the IDEA. No boxer

- **Translator and edition:** Aubrey Stewart, *Minor Dialogues, Together with the
  Dialogue on Clemency* (London: George Bell and Sons, 1889). The file's own header
  reads `Translator: Aubrey Stewart`, and its title page reads "LONDON — GEORGE BELL AND
  SONS, YORK STREET COVENT GARDEN 1889".
- **Page read:** https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/64576/pg64576.txt (whole work,
  sha256 `ff92ddd8…5c5a483b`, the same file hash as the 2026-10-02 De Ira check).
  Locators below are Stewart's own chapter numerals (I–VI) and his printed page
  numbers `{n}`. Modern sub-section numbers (e.g. 4.3–4) could not be checked
  against the Latin because Perseus was down.
- **House-translator note:** `06-sources.md` names no house translator for Seneca's
  dialogues. Stewart is the public-domain edition it points to for *De Beneficiis*,
  and the de facto one used for De Ira. **The author should confirm Stewart before
  anything is quoted.** I have not assumed it.
- **Wording on the page, strongest first:**
  - Ch. IV, p. 11, closest to "you don't know if you can take a punch":
    "I think you unhappy because you never have been unhappy: you have passed through your life without meeting an antagonist: no one will know your powers, not even you yourself. For a man cannot know himself without a trial: no one ever learnt what he could do without putting himself to the test;"
  - Ch. II, pp. 3–4, athletes who seek the strongest opponents:
    "We see athletes, who study only their bodily strength, engage in contests with the strongest of men, and insist that those who train them for the arena should put out their whole strength when practising with them: they endure blows and maltreatment, and, if they cannot find any single person who is their match, they engage with several at once: their strength and courage droop without an antagonist: they can only prove how great and how mighty it is by proving how much they can endure."
  - Ch. II, p. 4: "Unbroken prosperity cannot bear a single blow; but he who has waged an unceasing strife with his misfortunes has gained a thicker skin by his sufferings, yields to no disaster, and even though he fall yet fights on his knee."
  - Ch. III, p. 7, Demetrius: "“No one,” said he, “seems to me more unhappy than the man whom no misfortune has ever befallen.” He never has had an opportunity of testing himself;"
  - Ch. III, p. 7: "A gladiator deems it a disgrace to be matched with an inferior, and knows that to win without danger is to win without glory."
  - Ch. IV, p. 11: "you can judge of a pilot in a storm, of a soldier in a battle."
  - Ch. IV, p. 12: "The recruit turns pale at the thought of a wound: the veteran, who knows that he has often won the victory after losing blood, looks boldly at his own flowing gore."
  - Ch. IV, pp. 13–14: "Fortune lashes and mangles us: well, let us endure it: it is not cruelty, it is a struggle, in which the oftener we engage the braver we shall become. The strongest part of the body is that which is exercised by the most frequent use: we must entrust ourselves to fortune to be hardened by her against herself: by degrees she will make us a match for herself."
  - Ch. V, p. 17: "Fire tries gold, misfortune tries brave men."
- **Fit:** strong for the *idea* (an untested man cannot know himself; athletes
  "endure blows" and look for the strongest opponent). **No boxer, and no punch to
  the face.** The athletes in Ch. II are trainees who "endure blows and
  maltreatment". That is close, but it is not "being punched". The guesses in the
  brief all check out: athletes and strong opponents (II), fire proving gold (V),
  and the untested man not knowing his own strength (IV, plus Demetrius in III).
- **Recommended axes:** `evidence_source: page-text`, `status: verifiable` for
  Stewart's English as transcribed, pending the author's ruling on Stewart as the
  house translator. The Latin was not read.

### 3. Epictetus, *Discourses* I.24: NEAR. A sparring image, no blows

- **Translator and edition:** George Long, *The Discourses of Epictetus; with the
  Encheiridion and Fragments* (London: George Bell and Sons, 1877). This is the
  house edition. Wikisource's transcription of the title page reads "LONDON: GEORGE BELL AND
  SONS, YORK STREET, COVENT GARDEN. 1877."
- **Pages read:**
  - https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Discourses_of_Epictetus;_with_the_Encheiridion_and_Fragments/Book_1/Chapter_24
    (sha256 `6bea2c8c…3d39d392`). Chapter XXIV, "How we should struggle with
    circumstances", **p. 70** (scan 124), proofreading level 3.
  - Cross-checked against Project Gutenberg #10661 (Long's *Selection*), fetched
    from www.gutenberg.org (sha256 `7915eac7…fa3b2209`; header reads
    `Translator: George Long`). The GITenberg mirror on raw.githubusercontent.com
    is now **blocked (403)**: the probe confirmed it, and I did not route around it.
    gutenberg.org itself served the file. #10661 prints chapter titles but no book
    or chapter numbers. The I.24 locator comes from the numbered 1877 transcription.
- **Wording on the page (1877, Wikisource):** "It is circumstances (difficulties) which show what men are. Therefore when a difficulty falls upon you, remember that God, like a trainer of wrestlers, has matched you with a rough young man. For what purpose? you may say. Why that you may become an Olympic conqueror; but it is not accomplished without sweat. In my opinion no man has had a more profitable difficulty than you have had, if you choose to make use of it as an athlete would deal with a young antagonist."
- **Transcription variant, found by script diff:** the 1877 Wikisource text reads
  "Why that you may become", while Gutenberg #10661 reads "Why, that you may
  become". This is a one-comma disagreement between two transcriptions. Only the
  printed page settles it. The opening sentence, through "rough young man.", is
  identical in both.
- **Fit:** near. Difficulty "show[s] what men are", and the hard opponent is
  assigned to make you an Olympic victor. No one is struck, and the claim is that
  difficulty reveals and trains you, not that you can't know yourself without it.
  It pairs well with Seneca as a second voice.
- **Recommended axes:** `evidence_source: page-text`, `status: verifiable` (two
  agreeing transcriptions of Long, one keyed to the 1877 scan; the comma variant
  noted).

### 4. Epictetus, *Discourses* I.6 (Hercules): NEAR. Not in the brief, worth knowing

- **Edition:** Long 1877, as above. Page read:
  https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Discourses_of_Epictetus;_with_the_Encheiridion_and_Fragments/Book_1/Chapter_6
  (sha256 `2bd624a6…04bd28d2`), **p. 22** (scan 76). This passage is not in the
  Gutenberg *Selection*, so there is only one transcription.
- **Wording:** "What do you think that Hercules would have been if there had not been such a lion, and hydra, and stag, and boar, and certain unjust and bestial men, whom Hercules used to drive away and clear out? And what would he have been doing if there had been nothing of the kind? Is it not plain that he would have wrapped himself up and have slept? In the first place then he would not have been a Hercules, when he was dreaming away all his life in such luxury and ease; and even if he had been one, what would have been the use of him? and what the use of his arms, and of the strength of the other parts of his body, and his endurance and noble spirit, if such circumstances and occasions had not roused and exercised him?" … "they were useful for showing what Hercules was and for exercising him."
  *(two separate excerpts from the same paragraph; quote them separately, never
  joined with a bare ellipsis.)*
- **Fit:** near. Without the lion, there is no Hercules. Hardship shows and
  exercises strength. There is no blow and no fight sport.
- **Recommended axes:** `page-text`, `verifiable`.

### 5. Epictetus: taking blows, without the claim being tested

All Long 1877 (Wikisource), cross-checked by script against Gutenberg #10661. All
three are **identical** in both.

- *Enchiridion* XXIX, pp. 390–391 (scan 445 carries this sentence):
  "And sometimes you will strain the hand, put the ankle out of joint, swallow much dust, sometimes be flogged, and after all this be defeated.". Page:
  https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Discourses_of_Epictetus;_with_the_Encheiridion_and_Fragments/The_Encheiridion_or_Manual
  (sha256 `12ea7a55…7b18dac7`). **Fit: not this point.** Count the cost before
  you enter the contest, or you will quit like a child at play. *Discourses* III.15
  (pp. 234–235) is a near-duplicate of the same passage.
- *Discourses* III.10, pp. 222–223 (page break inside "receiving | blows"):
  "But this is just as if a man after receiving blows should give up the Pancratium. In the Pancratium it is in our power to desist and not to receive blows.". **Fit: no.** Philosophy is preparation, so don't quit when
  struck.
- *Discourses* III.25, p. 287: "For we must not shrink when we are engaged in the greatest combat, but we must even take blows.". **Fit: weak.** It is about not
  giving up, not about knowing yourself.
- Also checked: II.18 (pp. 158–162; "the true athlete … exercises himself against
  such appearances". Socrates conquered "not these sorry boxers". It is about
  impressions) and IV.10 (pp. 360–361; Hercules and the boar). **Neither supports
  the image.**
- **Recommended axes:** `page-text`, `verifiable` for any that are used. None is
  recommended for this purpose.

### 6. Marcus Aurelius: does not support it

- **Translator:** George Long. Gutenberg #15877, fetched from gutenberg.org (sha256
  `6584df7e…9cf88d5b`; header `Translator: George Long`). The file prints no
  publisher or date, so I cannot tie it from the file to Little, Brown 1889 (the
  house's named printing). The OKF records the same gap for other #15877 citations.
- VI.20: "In the gymnastic exercises suppose that a man has torn thee with his nails, and by dashing against thy head has inflicted a wound. Well, we neither show any signs of vexation, nor are we offended, nor do we suspect him afterwards as a treacherous fellow; and yet we are on our guard against him, not however as an enemy, nor yet with suspicion, but we quietly get out of his way. Something like this let thy behavior be in all the other parts of life; let us overlook many things in those who are like antagonists in the gymnasium. For it is in our power, as I said, to get out of the way, and to have no suspicion nor hatred." **Fit: no.** It is literally a blow to the head in sport,
  but the lesson is to bear no grudge, not that being struck tests you.
- VII.61: "The art of life is more like the wrestler's art than the dancer's, in respect of this, that it should stand ready and firm to meet onsets which are sudden and unexpected." **Fit: weak.** Readiness for sudden onsets.
- XII.9: "In the application of thy principles thou must be like the pancratiast, not like the gladiator; for the gladiator lets fall the sword which he uses and is killed; but the other always has his hand, and needs to do nothing else than use it." **Fit: no.** This is the "be a boxer, not a gladiator" line
  in other translations. It is about keeping your principles to hand.
