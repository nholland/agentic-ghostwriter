*Fact-Checker (gw-factchecker), 2026-10-02 11:45. One citation, worked as far up

## Verdict

**DEFECT: the quotation is not in De Ira.** It is not in II.3 or anywhere else in
the work, in Latin or in either public-domain English translation I could open.

- The Latin sentence the concept gives, *"Ratio affectibus tranquillitatem dare
  vult, non tollere,"* does not occur in Basore's Latin text of De Ira. I read all
  100 chapters (Books I to III) on Perseus. The word form *tranquillitatem* does
  not occur in the work at all.
- The English, *"Reason wishes to give calm to our emotions, not to root them
  out,"* does not occur in Aubrey Stewart's translation (Bell, 1889) or in
  L'Estrange's abstract (*Seneca's Morals*). Neither text contains any sentence
  close to it.
- **Worse than a wording problem: De Ira argues the opposite.** The only place
  the work uses *non tollere* is I.7.1. There Seneca reports the view of
  opponents ("quidam putant"): anger should be tempered, not removed. He then
  rejects that view. Book III ends (III.42.1) with *"exstirpemus radicitus … iram
  non temperemus, sed ex toto removeamus"*, which Stewart renders "extirpate root
  and branch … Let us not moderate anger, but get rid of it altogether." The
  chapter's line has Seneca saying "not to root them out." Seneca's own text says
  to root out.
- **Provenance.** The line has exactly one source, Kruse 2025 (*Psychology
  Today*). The book's saved copy of that article
  (`sources/articles/psychology-today-stoic-compassion.md`, line 113) gives both
  the Latin and the English with the locator "Book II, §3." I found no primary
  text behind it. A web search asked for the Latin repeated Kruse's attribution
  and locator as fact, without hedging. That is the confident search failure the
  evidence axis exists to catch.

**Recommended status: stays `unverified`.** I did read real pages, but what they
establish is that the quotation is *not* there. `verifiable` would claim that the
printed wording is located. It is not, and it cannot be.

**For the Publicist: this line cannot be quoted in a Substack post, in any
translation.** It has no translator, because Seneca never wrote it.

## What the pages actually say (copied from the fetched files by script, not retyped)

**De Ira II.3, the cited locator.** Seneca separates the involuntary first
movement from the passion. Nothing in it is about reason giving calm.

- Latin (Perseus/Basore, II.3.1): "Nihil ex his, quae animum fortuito impellunt,
  adfectus vocari debet; ista, ut ita dicam, patitur magis animus quam facit."
- Latin (II.3.4): "Hanc iram non voco, motum animi rationi parentem; illa est
  ira, quae rationem trans - silit, quae secum rapit." (The hyphen spacing is
  Perseus's line-break artefact.)
- Stewart, "Of Anger" Book II, section III, p. {79}: "A man may think himself
  injured, may wish to avenge his wrongs, and then may be persuaded by some
  reason or other to give up his intention and calm down: I do not call that
  anger, it is an emotion of the mind which is under the control of reason."

**De Ira I.7.1–2, where *non tollere* actually occurs.** This is the opponents'
view, and Seneca rejects it.

- Latin: "Optimum itaque quidam putant temperare iram, non tollere, …" /
  "Primum facilius est excludere perniciosa quam regere et non admittere quam
  admissa moderari;"
- Stewart, Book I, section VII, p. {56}: "Some therefore consider it to be best
  to control anger, not to banish it utterly, but to cut off its extravagances,
  and force it to keep within useful bounds," / "In the first place, it is
  easier to banish dangerous passions than to rule them; it is easier not to
  admit them than to keep them in order when admitted;"

**De Ira III.42.1, Seneca's own conclusion.**

- Latin: "Careamus hoc malo purgemusque mentem et exstirpemus radicitus, quae
  quamvis tenuia undecumque haeserint renascentur, et iram non temperemus, sed
  ex toto removeamus — quod enim malae rei temperamentum est?"
- Stewart, Book III, section XLII, p. {159}: "Let us be free from this evil, let
  us clear our minds of it, and extirpate root and branch a passion which grows
  again wherever the smallest particle of it finds a resting-place. Let us not
  moderate anger, but get rid of it altogether: what can moderation have to do
  with an evil habit?"

Also read: Stewart I.X, p. {60}: "wherefore a moderate passion is nothing but a
moderate evil."

## Proposed correction to Chapter 2 (for the author; I do not rewrite prose)

The chapter's argument holds: governance is not numbness. It just cannot rest
on this sentence. There are three honest routes, and all of them need the
author's ruling first:

**A. Real De Ira, Stewart (public domain), II.3.** This is the same locator the
chapter already claims. Stewart's sentence, read on the page:
"A man may think himself injured, may wish to avenge his wrongs, and then may
be persuaded by some reason or other to give up his intention and calm down: I
do not call that anger, it is an emotion of the mind which is under the control
of reason."

It could be shortened to the tail ("I do not call that anger, it is an emotion
of the mind which is under the control of reason"), but only as a marked
excerpt from one contiguous sentence. Two caveats:

- The sentence is about anger specifically, not "our emotions" in general.
- `06-sources.md` names no house translator for Seneca's dialogues. Stewart is
  the de facto public-domain choice (the Democritus concept makes the same
  point), and the author should confirm it.

**B. Real De Ira, Stewart, II.2/II.4: the "first movement" doctrine.** This is
the strongest true version of the chapter's point. Feeling the jolt is not the
failure; giving way to it is. Stewart II.II, p. {78}: "All these are emotions
of minds which are loth to be moved, and are not passions, but rudiments which
may grow into passions." Ch4 already uses II.4 (in Basore) for the three
stages, and Ch11's research note says De Ira "should not carry a third"
chapter. So route A or B should be weighed against that cap.

**C. No quotation.** Keep "Not suppress. Not eliminate. *Calm.*" as the
author's own words, and drop the Seneca attribution. This is the
lowest-risk fix and the one `06-sources.md` itself prefers when a passage
fights the register.

Letter 63 (Gummere, the house edition for the Letters: "we may weep, but we
must not wail") is already named in `ch02/research.md:48` as the secondary
option. I did not check it in this pass. It stays at whatever status its own
concept holds.
